      ###  Root cause analysis

      Once waste has been identified, the challenge is to discover why waste has occurred.

      Here you can use the 5 whys, where the simple question 'why' is asked over and over again until you can get to the root of a problem.

      This is known as root cause analysis.

      ### Root Cause Analysis – the five whys

      The 5 whys can be used to address single problems that occur in business by using a series of why questions.

      There will be times when posing the question once or twice gets to the heart of a problem. However there will be times when repeatedly asking the question brings results.

      Take a look below for an example:

      There are often three common sources of waste when you look to identify where it's coming from.  Scroll through the slides below to learn more about these.

    subInc:

          title: People
          text: |
            Often, waste is caused by the people in a business, and can occur because people make too many mistakes. As a result, time and effort must go into rectifying these mistakes. Mistakes can occur for all kinds of reasons, such as:
            * **Human error:** People know how to complete a task correctly but do something wrong in the process.  
            * **Lack of training:** People do not know how to do a task as a result of poor selection or training.
          img: ba_u2_s4_p2_slide_1.svg

          title: Materials and equipment
          text: |
            It is possible for waste to be caused by problems with materials and/or equipment, which in turn makes it likely that the products and services being produced are substandard and costly. Typically, waste can occur for a number of reasons, including:
            * Faults in raw materials
            * Faults in components supplied by an external organisation
            * Faults in components supplied from within the organisation
            * Machines or equipment breaking down or developing faults.
          img: ba_u2_s4_p2_slide_2.svg
        -
          title: Planning or processes
          text: |
            Even if people, materials and equipment are all functioning well, it's still possible for there to be significant levels of waste. This could be due to problems in the planning process. Here, managerial decisions can be the difference between efficiency and extreme waste. Typical examples could be:
            * Failing to learn about what customers really want
            * Over-supply or under-supply of products and services
            * Buying cheaper raw materials that lead to inferior and poor-quality products that don't pass quality tests
            * Failing to invest in the right areas of the business.

      ###  How you can minimise waste

      There are four broad approaches to minimising waste:
      1. Prevention
      2. Correction
      3. Identify waste and inform managers
      4. Use techniques such as **Lean Production**  

      Ideally, businesses should be developed so that little or no waste occurs, and staff should aim to minimise the waste they produce.

      However, it's rare that little or no waste occurs. For this reason there needs to be ways of correcting things and putting them right.

      Even the most efficient businesses produce waste. But the best businesses acknowledge their waste then seek to reduce it.
