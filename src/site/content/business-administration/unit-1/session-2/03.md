          title: Working Time Directive and Working Time Regulations (1998)
          copy: |
            The directive states that -

            - Your maximum weekly working time should be no more than 48 hours in seven days, unless you and your employer make an agreement in writing to the contrary.
            - A night shift should last no longer than 8 hours in any 24 hours
            - Work patterns should be monitored to protect worker’s health and safety. For example, if the work is monotonous an employer should ensure adequate rest breaks
            Employees should have a rest break of no less than 11 hours in every 24 hours
            - A weekly rest of no less than 24 hours uninterrupted should be ensured
            - A rest break of no less than 20 minutes should be provided if an adult’s working day is more than 6 hours.

            You need to know the legislation that applies to your job.
        - 
          title: UK Display Screen Equipment (DSE) Regulations
          copy: |
            The level of cover provided by these regulations depends on two factors - 

            1. The amount of time an employee spends using DSE. In order to qualify, employees must use DSE as a significant part of normal work.

            2. The employment status of workers. The regulations define employees as DSE "Users", while self-employed people are regarded as DSE "Operators". These distinctions are made on degree of control over work, level of financial risk, regularity of work and ownership of equipment. Operators are not covered by all aspects of the regulations, while users are.
        -
          title: European Directive
          copy: |
            ### What else do the DSE regulations say?

            In addition to the UK legislation, a European Directive on the subject states that - 

            1. Workers should have eye tests before starting display screen work; at regular intervals after that; if they experience visual difficulties.
            2. Employers should contribute towards the cost of these eye tests.
            3. Employers should regularly evaluate how healthy computer workstations are, on-site or otherwise.
            4. Employers should comply with minimum specified standards relating to screens, keyboards, work desks or space, work chairs, space, lighting, glare, noise, heat, radiation, humidity, ventilation and software.
            5. Workers should have adequate health and safety information about their workstation.
            6. Workers should receive training before starting any display unit work, and should take frequent, short breaks.
            7. There is also legislation that covers working time. Here employees need to control the number of consecutive days and hours people should work and they should ensure that there are adequate rest breaks.

          title: The Data Protection Act 1998
          text: |
            This Act requires all organisations that process data on individuals to be listed in the register of data controllers. Data is defined as information recorded or processed by computer and information that is part of a relevant filing system or forms part of an accessible record. An example of these is health records.

            Key points include -

            1. Data must be obtained and processed fairly and lawfully. Normally this means that individuals have given their consent. The more sensitive the data (such as that concerning religion or mental health), the more explicit the consent must be
            2 Data should be adequate, relevant and not excessive
            3. Personal data must be accurate, kept up to date and not kept for longer than is necessary
            4. Data must be securely stored to prevent unauthorised access, loss or destruction
            5. Data should not be transferred outside the EU unless the recipient country also protects data
            6. Data must be processed in accordance with the rights of individuals.
          img: BA_U1_S2_p2_Slider_1.svg

          title: More about the rights of individuals
          text: |
            Under the Data Protection Act, individuals have the right to - - Access data held about them
            - Prevent processing which would cause distress or damage
            - Prevent direct marketing usage
            - Automated decision making, such as credit checks
            - Address inaccurate information
            - Compensation if the Act is contravened.

            There are exemptions under the Act. This is for data related to things such as National security, Crime detection and Domestic use only.

            This legislation limits what can be done with information originated elsewhere. This is to protect the rights of people who create original work. The law applies to manuals, commercial documents, computer programs, articles, leaflets and books, as well as a host of other items including dramatic works, songs and artistic works.

            To use someone else’s work, you must get permission to do so. At times a fee may be involved. It's important to acknowledge when you have used someone else's work.
