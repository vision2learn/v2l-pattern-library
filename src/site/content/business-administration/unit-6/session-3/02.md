    title: Controlling budgets
    theme: t-bg-primary
    copy: |
      Once the budgets have been monitored and variances identified, the organisation will seek to find the causes.
      
      When variances are large, plentiful and / or have a significant impact on the efficient running of the organisation, the business operation may be potentially ‘out of control’. In this case, estimates could become meaningless.  
      
      Managers must take action. Select the images below to see some of the most common control actions.

    subInc: 
      type: reveal
      content:
        -
          title: Making adjustments to conform with an agreed budget
          copy: |
            Sometimes the budget managers will be able to tweak certain activities to get the budget back on track. This can directly affect the income and expenditure.
          img: placeholder_icon.svg
        -
          title: Identifying sources of wastage
          copy: |
            The removal of waste will always help to improve budgetary performance. Many organisations use processes such as lean management techniques to reduce waste.
          img: placeholder_icon.svg
        -
          title: Sourcing lower cost suppliers
          copy: |
            This may be done by finding alternative suppliers or making new arrangements with the current suppliers. By having better control of costs, the managers will have better control overall.
          img: placeholder_icon.svg
        -
          title: Identifying new customers or sales
          copy: |
            While the sales and marketing staff may have primary responsibility in this area, the whole organisation can try and identify new markets. This can help ensure that variances in budgets are more likely to be positive.
          img: placeholder_icon.svg
        -
          title: Developing more efficient work practices and decisions
          copy: |
            Continuously looking for ways to work more efficiently and make better decisions improves an organisation’s ability to run an efficient operation. This will manifest itself in the results that are seen in the budget, as they will be more likely to be beneficial for the organisation.
          img: placeholder_icon.svg
        -
          title: Sharing good practice
          copy: |
            If one business operation has found a way to cut costs, boost revenues or manage things more effectively, there may be lessons that can be shared throughout the organisation.
          img: placeholder_icon.svg

    title: Activity - Revisions to the budget
    theme: t-bg-supporting3
    copy: |
      Even the most thoroughly **prepared, researched and calculated** estimates may result in budgets which must be revised. Unforeseen changes may render the budget no longer applicable in its original state. When this happens, the budget will have to be revised.
      
      Try the activity below to see some of the external and internal changes which may influence a revision to the budget.

          title: Case study 1
          text: |
            ### Martha's cakes

            Martha runs a small catering company. She has a loan with a local bank.

            The Bank of England has recently raised interest rates by 0.5%. This means the interest rate on Martha’s loan goes up from 8.1% to 8.6%. This increases her repayments by £90 per month. 
            
            This equates to an unforeseen expenditure of £1,080 over the next 12 months.
            
          img: placeholder_icon.svg            
        - 
          title: Case study 2
          text: |
            ### A2B Deliveries
            
            The government raises the tax on fuel by 2p a litre.

            A2B Deliveries, a small local delivery service, uses 7,500 litres of petrol each month. The increase in tax will add an extra £150 per month to fuel costs. 
            
            This had not been anticipated in the budget.
          img: placeholder_icon.svg
        - 
          title: Case study 3
          text: |
            ### Stewarts Engineering Works
            
            An engineering factory assumed they would be able to extend their electricity and gas contract for a further eighteen months. However, changes in power costs meant that providers would not agree on such a deal. 
            
            Consequently, the new deal agreed with a new provider half way through the factory’s budget period saw an increase in energy costs of 7%. 
            
            This was something that had not been budgeted for.
