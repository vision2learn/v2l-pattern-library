### McClelland and Atkinson

  * ![McClelland and Atkinson](../../images/unit03/session03/pg6-graphic1.svg)

The McClelland and Atkinson approach sought to explain why some people appear to be more motivated to achieve than others. These psychologists observed two important components: 

    Intrinsic motivation: a person’s internal desire to do something 
    Drive to avoid failure. 

  * ![McClelland and Atkinson](../../images/unit03/session03/pg6-graphic2.svg)

In competitive situations the desire to succeed may be challenged by employees not wanting to be seen to fail. This can be represented in an equation:

**Desire to succeed – How afraid they are of not succeeding. = Achievement motivation **

  * ![McClelland and Atkinson](../../images/unit03/session03/pg6-graphic3.svg)

Therefore, an employee with a strong desire to succeed and no fears of failure will have a high achievement motivation. An employee with a moderate desire to succeed but a large fear of failure may have little or no motivation to work hard.

  * ![McClelland and Atkinson](../../images/unit03/session03/pg6-graphic4.svg)

#### Fear of success

Research on motivation has also found that some people are motivated by a fear of success. These people will avoid situations where they are likely to be successful or even ‘self-sabotage’ themselves if success looks possible.

## Hertzberg

Frederick Hertzberg looked at the relationship between motivation and job satisfaction in the workplace. He believed that there is a more complex relationship than just a manager motivating an employee. He identified two key factors: **satisfiers** and **dissatisfiers** which are listed in the box below.

Satisfiers Dissatisfiers (hygiene factors)

Achievement
Company policy and administration

Recognition
Supervision

Work itself
Salary

Responsibility
Interpersonal relations

Advancement
Working conditions 

The satisfiers motivate employees to perform well in the workplace. The dissatisfiers will lead to dissatisfaction if they are problematic in any way. Even if the dissatisfiers are only satisfactory, they will not motivate employees to do a better job.

## Expectancy theory

This theory of motivation explores the process of motivation and was developed by Vroom, Lawler and Porter. These motivation specialists looked at how people perceive the relationship between **effort**, **performance** and **reward**. They reasoned that employees are motivated to put in effort at work when they believe their **effort** will have a noticeable impact on **performance** and a belief that the performance will lead to **rewards**.

![Expectancy theory](../../images/unit03/session03/pg6-chart1.svg)

ClickTap to see some ways in which the relationship between effort, performance and rewards may fail to motivate employees.

1. Employee does not make effort __ for example the employee has minimal interest in their work and its outcomes.

2. Effort does not result in performance __ for example the employee lacks skills or adequate equipment.

3. Effective performance does not lead to rewards __ for example, there is no system for linking individual performance and rewards or the organisation does not have the resources to adequately reward individuals.

### In summary ![Summary](../../images/icons/icon-summary.svg)

There may be employees who fit McGregor’s theory X and others who fit theory Y. Some employees may be focusing on the lower levels of Maslow’s theory of needs while others focus on the higher levels. Some work environments may be lacking Hertzberg’s hygiene factors or motivators. Finally there may be employees believing in different effort / performance / reward relationships in an organisation. Remember, one theory does not fit all situations!
