# Applying motivation

The various theories show that different employees will be motivated in different ways. It is the team leader’s role to identify their team’s motivators and to take the appropriate action to try and establish them.

## Understanding team motivation

You have learnt about some of the common theories of motivation in this Session. It can be difficult for a team leader to work out the best way to motivate their team. The leader should research what motivates their team and how they can put it into place. Team leaders can use the following approach to understand how to motivate the team:

A. Ask team members what motivates them.

B. Based on the answer to question A the team leader can then apply one of the following questions: 

  1. Can action be directly taken by the team leader?
  2. Can action be directly taken by others – such as senior managers – within the organisation?
  3. Is action unable to be taken by anyone in the organisation?

### A = B1

Here, the motivations of each team member are within the control of the team leader. This means that the team leader has the ability to make decisions and take actions that will tap into the drive and enthusiasm of the team. 

If new staff are being selected for a team then one of the selection criterion may be that the person is strongly driven by the motivators that the team leader can deliver.

### A = B2

In this situation the things that motivate team members are not currently delivered by the team environment. However, they could be delivered by the organisation if senior managers wanted to do so.

If many team members would be motivated by changes, outside the power of the team leader, then they may discuss the situation with senior managers. 

Even if changes are unable to be made, team members will see that the issues have been raised. The senior managers will be aware of the mismatch between the motivators used by the organisation and the things which actually motivate the team.

### A = B3

In this case both the team leader and more senior managers in the organisation are currently powerless to provide what motivates team members. There is a mismatch between what employees want and what the organisation is offering. 

The organisation may need to rethink what it offers if the overwhelming majority of staff are not motivated by what is on offer.

The motivators for the team members that emerged from the questioning were:

  * Being treated with respect
  * Wanting to ‘belong’ to the team
  * Wanting flexibility in work hours as long as objectives were not compromised.

The leader’s strategy is to work with team members in each of the three areas and create a motivated team.

TapClick to see an example situation

The motivators for the team members that emerged from the questioning were:

  * Wanting flexibility in work hours as long as objectives were not compromised
  * Part of their payment based in skills and experience as other teams were often asking staff for advice
  * Post school crèche facilities for some of the team’s children.

These were motivators that were not controlled by the team leader in this organisation. The team leader could raise each issue with appropriate managers.

The senior managers may choose to make changes that will motivate the team, but they could decide that for the wider organisational needs and resources it is not the time to make any changes.

TapClick to see an example situation

The motivators for the team members that emerged from the questioning were:

  * Higher pay 
  * Bigger bonuses.

The team leader had no ability to satisfy these team members. Nor did the organisation have the resources to reward the team members to the level they desired.

The best the organisation and team leader could do was:

  * Try and find other motivators that could be addressed
  * Suggest that as things improve economically they could revisit the concerns of the team
  * Start considering how current team members could be replaced if they choose to leave.
