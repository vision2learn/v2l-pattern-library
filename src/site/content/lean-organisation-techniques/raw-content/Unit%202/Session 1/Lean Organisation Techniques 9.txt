At the heart of many Kaizen activities is the **elimination of waste**. 

Let’s look at what is meant by ‘waste’ in business and the impact that waste can have on productivity when it is not eliminated. 

When thinking about waste in the workplace, it is helpful to divide general work activities and business processes into three categories.

ClickTap each header to learn more. 

#### 1\. Value added

**Value added** work and processes include any changes to the product or service that increase the value of the output.

#### 2\. Non-value added

The term ‘**non-value added**’ is used to describe any necessary work activities and processes that do not add value to the output. Organisations aim to minimise the amount of non-value added activity without undermining the product or service. 

#### 3\. Waste 

Any unnecessary aspects of the business process, which do not add value, are called **waste**. Waste should be eliminated. 

* * *

### Eliminating waste

In a continuous improvement workplace, the aim is to identify and eliminate waste in order to lower costs and improve outputs. 

Taiichi Ohno, the chief engineer at Toyota, devised seven types of waste (known as Muda in Japanese). His classifications apply to both product and service industries. The classification system can help organisations to identify and eliminate waste, providing a focus for activity in the workplace. 

ClickTap through the slider to learn more. 

### Types of waste

The seven types of waste make the acronym ‘TIMWOOD’. Use it to help you remember them. 

  * ![Ian talking to a patient, and Jane standing with arms crossed](../../images/unit02/session01/pg9-transport.svg) 1\. Transport
  * #### Key aspects

    Poor layout
    Wrong position of raw materials
    Too much inventory
    Insufficiently sized storage areas

#### Benefits of elimination

    Efficient access to materials
    Reduced handling costs
    Reduced internal movement of materials
    Reduced risk of handling damage
    Reduced risk of health and safety hazards

  * ![Ian talking to a patient, and Jane standing with arms crossed](../../images/unit02/session01/pg9-inventory.svg) 2\. Inventory 
  * #### Key aspects

    Large stocks of work in progress
    Large stocks of raw materials
    Work-in-progress and raw materials cluttering the workplace

#### Benefits of elimination

    Money not tied up
    Workspaces not tied up
    Easier movement and transportation
    Lower potential hazards

  * ![Ian talking to a patient, and Jane standing with arms crossed](../../images/unit02/session01/pg9-motion.svg) 3\. Motion (by operators) 
  * #### Key aspects

    Too much stretching and bending to do the job
    Too much walking to get tools and materials etc.
    Unproductive work time
    Greater risk of injury

#### Benefits of elimination

    Reduction in injury and absenteeism
    Greater productivity
    Better environment and morale

  * ![Ian talking to a patient, and Jane standing with arms crossed](../../images/unit02/session01/pg9-waiting.svg) 4\. Waiting (idle time) 
  * #### Key aspects

    Some work areas working quicker/slower than others
    Different or conflicting cycle times
    Machines needing constant monitoring
    Delays in change over

#### Benefits of elimination

    Better use of operator time
    More efficient use of equipment
    Reduced likelihood of operator boredom with potentially fewer accidents as a result.

  * ![Ian talking to a patient, and Jane standing with arms crossed](../../images/unit02/session01/pg9-overproduction.svg) 5\. Overproduction 
  * #### Key aspects

    Creating an output that exceeds demand
    Producing outputs far ahead of schedule

#### Benefits of elimination

    Saves money on unnecessary production
    Exposes underlying causes of overproduction
    Cuts the risk of being left with stock that cannot be sold

  * ![Ian talking to a patient, and Jane standing with arms crossed](../../images/unit02/session01/pg9-over-processing.svg) 6\. Over-processing 
  * #### Key aspects

    Too much time spent on set-ups, change overs and down time
    Unproductive, unnecessary and complicated processes

#### Benefits of elimination

    Money saved by removing unnecessary processes
    Higher levels of equipment utilisation
    More efficient workflows

  * ![Ian talking to a patient, and Jane standing with arms crossed](../../images/unit02/session01/pg9-defects.svg) 7\. Defects (bad quality) 
  * #### Key aspects

    Planned overproduction to meet lower targets
    Large temporary storage areas/containers for rejects
    Excess overtime
    Increased customer complaints
    Additional resources needed for quality checks

#### Benefits of elimination

    Higher customer satisfaction
    Reduced rework costs
    Reduced waste disposal costs
    Lower overtime
    Reduced need for overproduction

### Case study ![Case study](../../images/icons/icon-case-study.svg)

Anna started a new job in a big design company. After working there for a few weeks, she noticed some problems with photocopying in the office. She found that people were making unnecessary copies, the photocopier was taking too long (and therefore queues were building) and it was unreliable. 

![](../../images/unit02/session01/pg9-casestudy.svg)

Anna recommended that the company buy a new, higher-quality photocopier and restrict the number of photocopies employees could make at any one time. The company took her advice on board, and found that they started to save money on paper and work activities started to speed up, enabling them to achieve more.
