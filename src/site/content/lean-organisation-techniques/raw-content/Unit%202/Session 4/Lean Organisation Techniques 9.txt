# Creating an SOP

In most organisations, the creation of a standard operating procedure is a **team activity.** The team is made up of employees who are actively involved in the processes that are being addressed by the SOP.

TapClick the image on the right to find out who might write SOPs.

![SOPs should be written by teams that include some, or all, of the following people.](../../images/unit02/session04/pg9-graphic1.svg)

SOPs should be written by teams that include some, or all, of the following people. 

**Those who will perform the job**

**Those who will perform maintenance on equipment involved in the SOP**

**Engineers or others who design equipment and processes**

**Technical writers**

**Safety personnel**

**Environmental personnel**

**Equipment manufacturers.**

  


Team writing ensures that comprehensive knowledge (acquired from different perspectives) is applied to the SOP. It also creates ‘buy-in’, which increases the likelihood that SOPs will be implemented under the guidance of the writers. Finally, it trains coaches – the people who write the SOP – and encourages employees to follow the SOP because they know the coaches invested time and effort on their behalf. 

Teams can write or edit parts of the SOP independently and then the contributions can be brought together. The draft SOP can then be circulated for further comments. Throughout the writing process, the team should be mindful of the health and safety implications of what they are creating.

The ideal time to create a SOP is **before** a new process is introduced within the organisation. However, it is possible that an organisation latterly recognises that SOPs may improve the way a process performs.

## Developing a procedure

Now that you have considered **who** is involved, let’s move on to consider **how** a standard operating procedure is developed. There are many elements that influence the process/sub-process for an SOP. 

The many sources and types of information required can be collected in a number of ways. Traditionally, ‘work study’ methods were used. More recently, it has become popular to video record a process and then have team members observe it in detail. 

  * ![Materials](../../images/unit02/session04/pg9-slide1.svg)

#### Materials 

The type of materials, times they are used, storage, ways they are moved and any health and safety implications must be considered. 

  * ![Process description](../../images/unit02/session04/pg9-slide2.svg)

#### Process description 

The inputs and outputs must be considered. 

  * ![Flow of work](../../images/unit02/session04/pg9-slide3.svg)

#### Flow of work

A knowledge of what is done, and when, how work in progress is stored, and bottlenecks must be considered. 

  * ![Layout of the area](../../images/unit02/session04/pg9-slide4.svg)

#### Layout of the area 

The size and shape of the floor space, the equipment, materials and location of people must be considered.

  * ![People](../../images/unit02/session04/pg9-slide5.svg)

#### People 

The number of people involved in the process and their roles must be considered. 

  * ![Actions](../../images/unit02/session04/pg9-slide6.svg)

#### Actions 

The kind of actions that occur, length of time they take to occur and location and time must be considered. 

  * ![Time taken](../../images/unit02/session04/pg9-slide7.svg)

#### Time taken

The time the process currently takes and known or anticipated upper and lower time limits must be considered.

  * ![Tools](../../images/unit02/session04/pg9-slide8.svg)

#### Tools 

The kind of tools that are used at each point, and knowledge of whether they are subject to change, must be considered. 

  * ![Key components](../../images/unit02/session04/pg9-slide9.svg)

#### Key components 

The characteristics, core points of the process and knowledge of how they got there should be considered. Collecting this information provides a fuller picture, making it possible to create a comprehensive standard operating procedure.

## Refining and improving the SOP

Finally, the SOP must be refined and improved. Click on the numbers below to learn about the final four steps in the process of creating an SOP. 

  * ![number 1](../../images/icons/icon-button1.svg)
  * ![number 2](../../images/icons/icon-button2.svg)
  * ![number 3](../../images/icons/icon-button3.svg)
  * ![number 3](../../images/icons/icon-button4.svg)

If the SOP appears to be turning into a very large document, it may be worth breaking it up. It may be that the large process can be conveniently sub-divided into a number of linked sub-processes, each with its own standard operating procedure.

As the SOP starts to be finalised, it is possible for team members to go beyond observation to reasonably question whether things could be done in ways that increase efficiency and/or reduce hazards. 

Another consideration is whether to have built-in **‘contingency actions’** which address what should be done if something goes wrong. Contingency actions often invoke shutting down the process, or part thereof, and informing specific people.

Finally, an agreed SOP is created. The date of creation and an issue number should be recorded. This is because SOPs are not destined to remain unchanged through time – they are subject to changes, and it is important that everyone works to the same, and most recent, SOP.
