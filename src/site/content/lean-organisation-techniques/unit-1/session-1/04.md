title: Types of waste
intro: |
  Perhaps the most distinctive aspect of lean management is the way that the concept of waste is split into seven distinct types of waste that must be reduced to increase efficiency. 

  Minimising these types of waste will make for a leaner work environment.

    title: Types of waste
    theme: t-bg-supporting2
    copy: |
      These seven types of waste were devised by Taiichi Ohno, an executive at Toyota who played an important role in the creation of TPS. They are known as the seven types of **muda**, which is the Japanese word for ‘waste’ or ‘wastefulness’.

      All seven types can be applied to the service industry just as much as they can to the manufacturing industry.

      Select through the slides below to learn more about the seven types of waste:

    subInc: 
      type: slider
      title: Waste
      slides:
        -
          title: Transportation
          text: |
            When products and materials are moved unnecessarily due to poor layout, there is too much stock to transport or there are insufficiently sized storage areas.

            ### Case study  
            Working from two offices in two different cities, a stationery supplies company often has to send invoice forms between offices which takes up valuable time and costs money for postage.
          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p4_Transport.svg
        -
          title: Inventory
          text: |
            If a business finds itself with large stocks of work in progress or large stocks of raw materials then they can clutter the workplace.

            ### Case study

            Sam has recently opened Sam’s Deli in the centre of town, selling freshly made sandwiches and other snacks. Despite a successful opening week, Sam’s business inexperience has caused him to over order on some ingredients and under order on others. As a result, a whole batch of tomatoes has gone off while the popular turkey sandwich has had to be taken off the menu as he’s run out of turkey. 

          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p4_Inventory.svg
        - 
          title: Motion
          text: |
            Unnecessary movements such as too much stretching or bending and walking for tools and materials can mean work time is totally unproductive and puts workers at greater risk of injury.

            ### Case study

            For example, an office may have a post room on the second floor of the building when all letters and correspondence are prepared on the first floor. Moving the post up and downstairs may be wasting precious work time that adds up over a long period of time. 

          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p4_Motion.svg
        -
          title: Waiting
          text: |
            Time can be wasted when waiting for the next step in the process as a result of some areas working quicker or slower than others, machines needing constant monitoring or if there are delays in changeovers.

            ### Case study

            A factory that manufactures specially designed bikes has quality controls in place that ensure each part is made to the highest standard. However, production is often held up when a new frame is tested for durability leaving others further along the manufacturing chain with nothing to do.

          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p4_Waiting.svg
        -
          title: Overproduction
          text: |
            Waste can occur if production outputs exceed demand or if production is far too ahead of schedule.

            ### Case study

            Bertha’s Bonnie Jams have managed to secure an order from a local supermarket of 300 jars of their gourmet jam. They decide to make another 600 jars on top of the initial 300 as they fully expect the supermarket to order even more. Unfortunately, no more orders are forthcoming so instead the jars are left to sit in a warehouse unsold and eventually have to be thrown away.

          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p4_Overproduction.svg
        -
          title: Overprocessing
          text: |
            Outputs that require more work or are of a higher quality than is required can create unnecessary work with processes that are unproductive or too complicated.
            ### Case study

            When launching a new variety of crisps, a small snack company decides to commission an artist to personally draw, by hand, each individual packet. While the packets look attractive, most consumers barely pay any attention to the packets which end up being thrown away once the crisps have been eaten! 

          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p4_Overprocessing.svg
        -
          title: Defects
          text: |
            When outputs need to be reworked or scrapped then waste can occur as a result of extra quality checks, storage for rejects, increased customer complaints and excess overtime.

            ### Case study

            A local sofa company has printed off 1000 leaflets to post through people’s doors in the area but just as they’re about to be sent off an employee notices a major spelling error in the tagline. The company has no option but to dispose of the leaflets and print another 1000 with the typo corrected at great cost.

          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p4_Defects.svg

    copy: |
      In a workplace that is focused on continuous improvement, the main aim is to identify and eliminate waste. The idea is that by always looking for ways to improve and lower costs, improved outputs will inevitably occur.

      Select through the slides below to see how a traditional organisation compares to a lean organisation.

    subInc:
      type: slider
      title: Traditional vs Lean
      slides:
        -
          title: Traditional organisation
          text: |
            1.  **Business focus**:  More concerned with the product or service. Traditional organisations are more interested in keeping things stable and predictable.
            2. **Organisation structure**:  Hierarchical with a more traditional power pyramid. Bosses at the top for a more top-down form of decision making and problem solving.
            3. **Operational activities**:  Usually have a set and rigid structure that has remained the same over a number of years as there are few problem solving techniques that attempt to improve how things are done or get to the bottom of why certain things aren’t working.

          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p4_TraditionalOrg.svg
        -
          title: Lean organisation
          text: |
            1. **Business focus**: Far more customer centred. Geared toward identifying potential improvements and changes that help fulfil what the customer wants and needs to a higher standard.
            2. **Organisation structure**:  For lean organisations, the power structure is flatter with less emphasis on those at the top dictating choices and decisions. Flexibility is encouraged, as are individual initiatives and suggestions. 
            3. **Operational activities**:  Employees participate on work activities with greater fluidity. Greater emphasis on experimentation and testing out hypotheses about improving activities.
          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p4_LeanOrg.svg

    title: summary
    theme: t-bg-primary i-summary
    copy: |
      In comparison to more traditional organisations, businesses that implement lean management techniques are more fluid in the way they work – providing more opportunities for people to express ideas and opinions about the way things could be improved.

      As lean organisations are continually looking for ways to improve, they are less likely to grow stale and are better able to identify and iron out any problems with their processes.
