          title: Toyota Production System
          text: Lean manufacturing is a management philosophy that has been adapted from the Toyota Production System (TPS) which was originally created by Toyota’s founder Sakichi Toyoda and developed between 1948 and 1975.
          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p3_slider_history_1.svg
        -
          title: Waste
          text: |
            TPS focused on creating a system where waste and inconsistency are kept to a minimum, encouraging a manufacturing process that is smooth and flexible. It wasn’t until the 1990s that the process was properly adapted and identified as ‘lean’.
          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p3_slider_history_2.svg
        - 
          title: Triumph of the Lean Production System
          text: |
            John Krafcik in his 1988 article “Triumph of the Lean Production System” was the first person to use the term ‘lean’ after coming into contact with TPS working as a quality engineer in a joint venture with Toyota.
          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p3_slider_history_3.svg
        -
          title: The Machine that Changed the World
          text: |
            The process was further developed by James Womack, Daniel Jones, and Daniel Roos in a book called _The Machine that Changed the World_ which made the term ‘lean management’ known worldwide.
          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p3_slider_history_4.svg
        -
          title: Expansion
          text: |
            Since then, the principles and philosophies about waste reduction and lean management has expanded out of the car manufacturing industry having been adapted and applied across a wide range of industries and businesses.

      Please see below for the key lean terms:

    subInc: 
      type: accordion
      content:
        - 
          title: Value added
          copy: |
            In terms of business, value added has two main aspects. Firstly, an activity or process that changes the product or service so that it is more in line with what the customer wants is considered to add value. Secondly, the focus should be on getting something right first time so that resources aren’t wasted trying to correct mistakes or problems.
        - 
          title: Non-Value added
          copy: |
            Necessary work under current work activities and processes that do not add any value. As this work comes at a cost to the organisation, the aim is to minimise the amount of non-value added activity while still trying not to undermine or affect the quality of product or service. 
        - 
          title: Waste
          copy: |
            Any unnecessary aspect of the business process that doesn’t add value. There are seven different sources of waste that lean management techniques have identified and that need to be eliminated. These are transportation, waiting, defects, motion, overproduction, inventory and overprocessing. You’ll learn more about each of these on the next page.
