intro: The lean approach to manufacturing and business organisation focuses on creating **more value with less work**. It sees the expenditure of resources on any goal other than the creation of value for the end customer to be wasteful. By eliminating this waste, the organisation becomes leaner.

          title: Toyota Production System
          text: Lean manufacturing is a management philosophy that has been adapted from the Toyota Production System (TPS) which was originally created by Toyota’s founder Sakichi Toyoda and developed between 1948 and 1975.
          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p3_slider_history_1.svg
        -
          title: Waste
          text: |
            TPS focused on creating a system where waste and inconsistency are kept to a minimum, encouraging a manufacturing process that is smooth and flexible. It wasn’t until the 1990s that the process was properly adapted and identified as ‘lean’.
          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p3_slider_history_2.svg
        - 
          title: Triumph of the Lean Production System
          text: |
            John Krafcik in his 1988 article “Triumph of the Lean Production System” was the first person to use the term ‘lean’ after coming into contact with TPS working as a quality engineer in a joint venture with Toyota.
          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p3_slider_history_3.svg
        -
          title: The Machine that Changed the World
          text: |
            The process was further developed by James Womack, Daniel Jones, and Daniel Roos in a book called _The Machine that Changed the World_ which made the term ‘lean management’ known worldwide.
          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p3_slider_history_4.svg
        -
          title: Expansion
          text: |
            Since then, the principles and philosophies about waste reduction and lean management has expanded out of the car manufacturing industry having been adapted and applied across a wide range of industries and businesses. 
          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S1_p3_slider_history_5.svg

    title: Key Lean Terms
    theme: t-bg-primary
    copy: |
      Please see below for the key lean terms:

    subInc: 
      type: accordion
      content:
        - 
          title: Value added
          copy: |
            In terms of business, value added has two main aspects. Firstly, an activity or process that changes the product or service so that it is more in line with what the customer wants is considered to add value. Secondly, the focus should be on getting something right first time so that resources aren’t wasted trying to correct mistakes or problems.
          img: /images/svg/PLACEHOLDER_Icon.svg
        - 
          title: Non-Value added
          copy: |
            Necessary work under current work activities and processes that do not add any value. As this work comes at a cost to the organisation, the aim is to minimise the amount of non-value added activity while still trying not to undermine or affect the quality of product or service.
          img: /images/svg/PLACEHOLDER_Icon.svg
        - 
          title: Waste
          copy: |
            Any unnecessary aspect of the business process that doesn’t add value. There are seven different sources of waste that lean management techniques have identified and that need to be eliminated. These are transportation, waiting, defects, motion, overproduction, inventory and overprocessing. You’ll learn more about each of these on the next page.

    title: Principles of lean management
    theme: t-bg-supporting3
    copy: |
      The philosophy of lean management and manufacturing is a generic set of ideas that have been adapted to fit different kinds of manufacturing and business processes.

      Across all these different businesses, attempts to improve productivity and quality using lean organisation techniques have the same set of principles at heart.

      See below to learn more about the five principles of lean management.

    subInc: 
      type: accordion
      content:
        - 
          title: 1. Specify what creates value for customers
          copy: |
            Here, value is the quality of service provided to the end customer. Only a small fraction of the time and effort spent working in a business directly adds value for the customer. By specifying activities in the work process that add value for the customer, it becomes easier to identify non-value activities that could be minimised or removed.
        - 
          title: 2. Identify steps along the process chain
          copy: |
            The process chain contains all the activities and processes that help deliver the product or service to the end customer. This represents, from start to finish, how value is delivered to the customer.
        - 
          title: 3. Make processes flow by eliminating waste
          copy: |
            Once activities and processes have been identified, the challenge is then to find ways to remove or minimise non-value waste. You’ll learn about the seven different types of waste later in this Session. 
        - 
          title: 4. Respond to customer demand 
          copy: |
            Check what the customer wants so that the business produces what is wanted exactly when the customer wants it. This can help to make sure that any changes and attempts to minimise waste are working. 
        - 
          title: 5. Strive for perfection by continual improvement
          copy: |
            A lean business that doesn’t want to stand still must constantly be on the lookout for ways to improve. The aim is to introduce lean management techniques throughout the business so that the employees are always looking for ways to improve efficiency and minimise waste.
