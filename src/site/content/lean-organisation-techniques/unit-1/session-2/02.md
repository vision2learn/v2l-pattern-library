          title: Case study 1
          copy: |
            Amy’s Authentic Americano is a small, high street coffee shop. Amy’s store has been open for 6 months and she has decided to bring out an iced coffee drink for the summer. The new iced drink has been available to her customers for a month now and Amy wishes to assess the productivity of her new drink campaign. 

            She collects feedback on the beverage from her customers to measure customer satisfaction with the product. Generally, the feedback is positive and many people have been impressed with the new drink. However, Amy notices that those who left feedback after having the drink on a Sunday have not been as impressed. 

            Two people work in the shop alongside Amy. Zheng has worked with Amy since the shop opened and Yusuf is new to the team. Yusuf only works on Sundays and Amy suspects his inexperience is leading to a poorer quality iced coffee drink being made. 

            Here we can see an example of how Amy has attempted to **measure productivity**. She has used her **customers** as an area of interest, measuring **satisfaction** and **dissatisfaction** of her product through feedback. Additionally, Amy has looked at the **quality** of her product and has measured the **source of the defect** in how the drink is being prepared. This now gives her a basis to act and ultimately improve the productivity of her business.

          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U1_S2_P2_Casestudy_1.svg

        - 
          title: Case study 2
          copy: |
             well-established engineering company, Enhanced Engines, supplies specialised engine parts to construction companies across the region. The construction companies rely on the engineering company’s produce, meaning that the engineering company is always in high demand. Nevertheless, Enhanced Engines are looking to make their organisation lean and improve their productivity. The company feels it could be more productive in its main cost expenditure area, delivery. 

              Enhanced Engines make deliveries 7 days a week, with as many as 100 separate deliveries each day! The company owns 6 delivery vehicles and employs 14 drivers altogether. However, out of the 14 only 6 people are employed full time. The managing director, Keith, of Enhanced Engines looks at the delivery records for the past 3 months. Keith notices that there is a severe lack of consistency in many of the regular journeys the delivery drivers make and suspects this is largely due to the delivery drivers themselves. The company has a 95% success rate of on-time deliveries over some periods but only a 75% success rate in other periods. Customers having to suffer longer waits are harmful to the relationships Enhanced Engines has with its buyers. 

              Here we can see how Keith has attempted to **measure productivity**. Where Amy looked at customer satisfaction, Keith has focused on **delivery** as the principal area of interest. He has measured productivity by looking at whether deliveries have been **on time** as well as considering **customer wait time**. Due to delivery being one of the most essential areas of Enhanced Engines’ business, it is logical for Keith to measure productivity in this area. He now has a basis to act as he has determined an area where productivity can be improved.

    title: Selecting the appropriate measures
    theme: t-bg-supporting3
    copy: |
      ### Which measures should be prioritised?

      When prioritising measures, organisations and people within these organisations should follow the **four step** approach. This way, consistent approaches will be used throughout the organisation. 

      ### The four steps of priority

      Remember to **think four** when talking about how organisations prioritise measures. 

      1. Determine the **organisational** objectives and state them in measurable and time bound terms. This may require a period of collecting the views of stakeholders, including customers, and then deciding upon the key objectives. The objectives should not conflict. If some are more important than others this should be clearly presented.
      2. Identify and prioritise the **organisational processes** that deliver the outcomes in step 1. Specify the measures used to evaluate the outputs of these processes. 
      3. Where applicable, based on the desired process outcomes, identify and prioritise **team objectives** and the methods used to measure performance. 
      4. Where applicable, based on desired process and / or team objectives, identify and prioritise **individual objectives** and the methods used to measure performance. 

      By following the **four step** approach, measures are selected and prioritised consistently.  The most important measures are most likely to contribute to increasing productivity and other important organisation objectives.

      For a lean organisation, the areas identified using the four steps will also be those areas where the elimination of waste will result in improved performance and a greater likelihood that objectives will be met.
