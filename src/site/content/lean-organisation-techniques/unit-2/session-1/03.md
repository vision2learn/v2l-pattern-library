title: Kaizen
intro: | 
  An organisation can develop a mind-set and approach that seeks to continuously find and implement improvements.

  There has been a significant commitment to **continuous improvement** in Japan. During the post-World War II period, American business experts like William Shewhart and W.E. Deming shared their ideas with Japanese industrialists. A significant outcome of this union was the emergence of **Kaizen**.

    title: What is Kaizen?
    theme: t-bg-primary
    copy: |
      The term ‘**Kaizen**’ means ‘gradual and orderly continuous improvement’. It comes from the words ‘kai’ (which means ‘continuous’) and ‘zen’ (which means ‘good’). 

      In Japan, the word carries the meaning of **change, renewal and spirit**. Adopting a Kaizen approach in the workplace brings a spirit of renewal. This means the organisation should operate within a culture where change and continuous improvement are the norm. 

      ### The four principles of Kaizen

    title: Summary
    theme: t-bg-primary i-summary
    copy: |
      The term ‘Kaizen’ means ‘gradual and orderly continuous improvement’. 
      
      Adopting a Kaizen approach in the workplace means that the organisation should operate within a culture where change and continuous improvement are the norm. Kaizen is a new way of thinking. 
      
      Decisions about day-to-day activities may be made by the people doing the activities, rather than by the managers at the top of the organisation. Therefore, information flows both up and down and back and forth throughout the organisation, rather than simply from the top-downwards.

    title: New thinking approaches
    theme: 
    copy: |
      Traditionally, organisations are hierarchical – all important ideas and changes come down from those who have power and authority to those who have none. 

      The Kaizen approach is a new thinking approach. It challenges many of the traditional and common ideas about how organisations operate.

    title: The benefits of new thinking
    theme: 
    copy: |
      New thinking organisations recognise that thousands of activities and decisions occur every day. It is unrealistic to expect a few managers to understand and improve them all. However, if the people who participate in these activities and decisions are trained in the use of continuous improvement techniques, then many small changes can result in substantial improvements to the organisation.

      Changes should not be made in isolation. The horizontal and vertical flow of information and communication is important. It is necessary to know how every change will affect colleagues, suppliers and customers. Therefore, any negative effects can be quickly detected and changed.

      By adopting the new way of thinking, employees can see the ways they influence their organisation. They are able to produce a product or service then discover ways to improve it.

    title: Five continuous improvement questions
    theme: t-bg-primary
    copy: |
      At all times, the organisation and its staff should attempt to look at QCD and the seven Department of Trade and Industry (DTI) measures. The organisation should also be sensitive to the potential impact a change in one area may have on the performance of another. This is why the vertical flow of information is so important. 

      See below the five questions that any organisation committed to continuous improvement should ask regularly.
    subInc: 
      type: accordion
      content:
        - 
          title: Five questions
          copy: |
            1. Are the changes too big or small? 
            2. Are the right people involved in the changes?
            3. Is the necessary documentation and training available?
            4. Are our improvement priorities right?
            5. Are we focused on adding value for customers?

            As you can see, any organisation that is committed to continuous improvement thinks and operates in very different ways to traditional businesses. The new approach requires information to be communicated more freely, and for staff to be trained and empowered to make more decisions. This requires a lot of time, effort and resources. However, the benefits to individuals, the organisation and the economy as a whole may be sizable.
