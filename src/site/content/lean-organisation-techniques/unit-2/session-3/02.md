    title: Guiding principles of visual management
    theme: t-bg-primary
    copy: |
      In order to choose appropriate visual indicators, it’s important to carefully consider exactly what outcomes the business requires.

      Scroll through the slides below to see some of these principles in more detail. 
    subInc:
      type: slider
      title: 
      slides:
        -
          title: Correct
          text: First and foremost, any visual information should be correct. Incorrect visual indicators can come in all forms, from incorrectly labelled shelves to directional arrows that point the wrong way. Clearly, if visual methods are incorrect they can add extra time onto work processes, cause employees to not trust what is being displayed and even be a hazard to health.

          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U2_S3_P2_Slide_1.svg
        -
          title: Relevant
          text: Providing target figures and work feedback from a different department isn’t going to be much use if it has no impact on someone’s work. In a more extreme example, something like a tyre pressure indicator that displays the temperature of the room is hardly going to be any use to a car mechanic!

          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U2_S3_P2_Slide_2.svg
        -
          title: Important
          text: Visual information and feedback must be carefully chosen so only the most important information is displayed. Displaying irrelevant or unimportant information can not only cause employees to not pay attention to signs, displays and charts but can also cause them not to pay attention to the really important stuff as well! 

          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U2_S3_P2_Slide_3.svg
        -
          title: Keeps people informed
          text: By visibly displaying any changes, updates or modifications to work processes you can make sure that all employees are kept informed with the most up-to-date information. For example, if the place where A4 photocopier paper is stored changes, then visual indicators relating to this should be changed as soon as possible.

          img: /LeanManagement/LM_U2_S3_P2_Slide_4.svg

    title: Summary
    theme: t-bg-primary i-summary
    copy: |
      While visual management may sound like a rather simple aspect of lean, it can actually be quite a tricky thing to get right. All visual indicators must be carefully chosen so that they add value to the work environment and work processes.
      
      Poorly chosen visual indicators can have a negative impact on the workplace as employees end up thinking that the information is not under their control. They may not understand the information or they might think it is irrelevant.

    title: Real-life visual indicators
    theme: t-bg-primary1a i-case-study
    copy: |
      All businesses who wish to continually improve must focus on controlling **health** and **safety** as well as **quality**, **cost** and **delivery**. Effective visual indicators can help when it comes to the control and feedback of these five factors.

      However, in order to be effective, there should be a balance between visual and verbal indicators.

      Read the case studies on this page to see how this might work in real work environments.

    subInc: 
      type: accordion
      content:
        - 
          title: Case study 1
          copy: |
            On a white goods assembly line, there were hold-ups due to the accessibility of a particular component. To address this problem, a square-shaped area was first ‘taped’ off and later the lines were painted. The area was in a safe location. It was also convenient for the assembly team. The square was subdivided into two halves. When half the square was empty the team knew it was time to request the delivery of more components to their work area. Staff were trained that the components were stored within the designated area. This approach would be reviewed in three months time. Further changes would be made if appropriate.
          img: /images/svg/LeanManagement/LM_U2_S3_P2_CaseStudy_1.svg
        - 
          title: Case study 2
          copy: |
            A company delivering farming equipment had occasional problems with delivery times. They discovered that the lorries were running out of petrol with less experienced drivers. These drivers waited until the petrol light went on before re-fuelling. However, if the light went on in remote country areas, there were no petrol stations available.
          img: /images/svg/LeanManagement/LM_U2_S3_P2_CaseStudy_2.svg
        - 
          title: Case study 3
          copy: |
            On a production line, the team were told weekly what the rejects per thousand figures had been. Rarely could they remember what had happened the week earlier and they had no understanding of how well they were doing. Also, if the figure appeared high, they waited a week to discover a problem. It was decided to plot the average reject figure per week and show the figures over time. This was updated weekly and the staff could now see trends. They then moved to daily figures that made appropriate analysis and adjustments possible. It was intended to see if even more regular feedback could improve performance. The time series was updated daily by the team leader and pinned on the team notice board.
          img: /images/svg/LeanManagement/LM_U2_S3_P2_CaseStudy_3.svg

    title: Visual management techniques
    theme: t-bg-supporting3
    copy: |
      Different businesses have different VMT requirements as their inputs, processes and outputs differ.

      See below to learn more.
    subInc: 
      type: accordion
      content:
        - 
          title: Inputs - People
          copy: |
            VMTs for employees should focus on presenting them with relevant information that helps them to do their job effectively. Visual indicators such as photos of fire wardens and holiday charts are easily overlooked but, when implemented properly, can have a very real impact. 

            Examples include:

            * Photographs of team members
            * Skills matrix
            * Holiday chart

        - 
          title: Inputs - Machines
          copy: |
            Various VMTs can help ensure machinery is operated correctly and efficiently. Clear, visual data can prevent improper operation and cut down on damage and failure. This approach isn’t just relevant for manufacturing either; it can be applied to any piece of machinery in the workplace including computers, phones and even communal fridges! 

            Examples include:

            * Shadow boards
            * 5C check sheet
            * Maximum / minimum levels on dials
        - 
          title: Inputs - Materials
          copy: |
            When thinking about VMTs in the work environment, visual indicators on materials are possibly the first thing that comes to mind. Properly labelling materials and where they should go is both practical and good practice for health and safety. Whilst inventory indicators help to keep stocks consistent and prevents important tools and materials from running out. 

            Examples include: 
            * Maximum / minimum level markers
            * Inventory indicators
            * Location areas
            * Material identification
        - 
          title: Process
          copy: |
            A crucial aspect of lean management is making work processes flow better so that they’re more efficient. VMTs relating to process can help by standardising how work is carried out and sharing best practice with all employees. Examples include easily understood operating instructions as well as clear safety displays. 

            Examples include:

            * Standard operations
            * Operation / control limits
            * Visual aids / photographs of acceptable outputs

        - 
          title: Outputs
          copy: |
            Outputs can be any outcome or service that the business provides. For example, the output at a car manufacturing plant will be completed cars off the production line, whilst in an IT department output could be completed service calls or repairs. Effective VMTs can keep employees updated on how the business is performing and encourage them to adapt their work processes if necessary. 

            Examples include:

            * Sales charts
            * Productivity graphs
            * Customer satisfaction numbers
