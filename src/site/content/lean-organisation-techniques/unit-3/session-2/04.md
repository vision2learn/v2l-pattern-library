    title: Why are objectives important to reviews
    theme: t-bg-supporting2
    layout: media
    copy: |
      Here are a few of the reasons why objectives are important to reviews:
      * When a team has SMART objectives, performance can be reviewed objectively.
      * It can be determined whether or not an objective has been achieved. 
      * Team objectives make it possible to plan out what needs to be done. Team members can then be informed about what they should do and how performance may be measured and reviewed.
      * The organisation can produce job descriptions which form a basis for job appraisals. Job descriptions can also be used by team members to self-evaluate.

    title: Case study - Team effectiveness
    theme: t-bg-primary1a i-case-study
    copy: |
      There may be measurable tasks undertaken by individuals. These tasks may relate to ‘hard measures’ and ‘soft measures’.

      Each team member should know how his or her progress will be monitored in terms of **measurable results** and those which may be observed by colleagues, team leaders or others. The contribution of a team member may be understood in terms of **technical skills, social skills** and **team roles.**

      Read the two case studies below and consider the differences between them, then read the conclusion.
    subInc: 
      type: accordion
      content:
        - 
          title: Swidgets & Co.
          copy: |
            In Traditional Swidgets & Co.’s manufacturing factory, one assembly team has no specific goals. Employees are simply expected to work as hard as possible and get products ‘out of the door’. There are no systematic instructions and people generally do what they think is best. No steps have been taken to check details and understanding. Teams are never certain whether they are doing well, as goals and appropriate performance measures for the team are never specified. Nor can individual performance be assessed, as individual performance measures are also absent. With no reviews of performance it becomes virtually impossible to systematically map out ways that things may be improved at any level.

            In this scenario, everyone may be well intentioned, intelligent and motivated yet it would be very difficult to systematically deliver improving outputs. The team will be made up of individuals with differing views about what the goals, structures, plans and roles should be. There could be endless disagreements on what should be done and the team would find it difficult to put things right.
          img: /images/svg/LeanManagement/lm_u3_s2_p4_casestudy_1.svg
        - 
          title: Super Swidgets & Co.
          copy: |
            On an assembly line a team are tasked with the assembly of a small cutting tool with 20 components. The key performance measures for the assembly team are:

            * Assemble 1000 cutting tools per week.
            * Have a quality assurance rejection rate / fault return rate of 1 in 5000 maximum.
            * Have a ‘waste’ in assembly maximum of 0.1% or less.

            Week on week, month on month and annually the measures are taken and available to the assembly team. There is an ongoing drive within the organisation to improve performance. When a target or objective is not met then the team are asked why. A recent underperformance by the team has been attributable to the following causes:

            * External supplier failure in terms of the quality of a component.
            * Internal supplier failure in terms of a delay in delivering a component prepared by another team.
            * Equipment used by the team malfunctioned.
            * A sub team responsible for the final stage of the assembly were completing 185 rather than 200 units per day. Within a week this resulted in only 925 products being assembled and 75 units’ worth of additional work in progress being stockpiled in the team floor space.

            Issues 1 and 2 are supplier issues, in both cases the team leader communicated with the appropriate people outside the team and this is taken into account by the organisation.

            Issues 3 and 4 are internal issues and it was found that:

            * There had been a failing in terms of individual performance. The team member responsible for checking the failed piece of equipment and reporting problems to maintenance had failed to do so.
            * A closer look at the assembly sub team showed that three of the four team members were performing very well, while the fourth member was the source of the underperformance. Instead of completing 50 units a day he was managing only 35.
          img: /images/svg/LeanManagement/lm_u3_s2_p4_casestudy_2.svg
        - 
          title: Conclusion
          copy: |
            Super Swidgets & Co. now has objectives and targets. This will allow it to collect relevant data which can be analysed and reviewed. When this is complete the organisation should be able to take appropriate action to deal with any issues found and help prevent them happening in the future.

            In the case described above they may consult with the two team members who’s shortcomings in this case greatly impacted the teams overall productivity. By dealing with these individuals team effectiveness may visibly improve.

    title: Self-management
    theme: t-bg-supporting2
    copy: |
      The effectiveness of a team is also partly dependent on **self-management**. Self-management should be reviewed alongside team and individual contributions. 

      It helps to think of three simple phrases: 
      1. Plan it
      2. Do it
      3. Review it

      There are several methods and techniques that may be used to help employees manage their time – and themselves – effectively. Three of the most commonly used are **planners, to-do lists** and **diaries**.

      Scroll through the slides below learn more about each tool. 

    subInc: 
      type: slider

      slides:
        -
          title: Planners
          text: |
              Once an individual has planned out their potential tasks and activities, they should be able to set **priorities**. They should also be able to calculate how much time will be required to do each task. With this information, they can then plan their day and week, and create a written document that maps out what they will do, when they will do it and how long it should take. Once the plan has been drafted, it makes sense to check it is acceptable to a supervisor or manager. 

          img: LeanManagement/lm_u3_s2_p4_silde_10.svg

          title: To do lists
          text: |
              A quick and easy technique is to take work tasks and list them in order of priority. The task with the highest priority goes at the top of the list and the tasks diminish in priority as they move down the list. Once the list has been written, the individual simply works their way through the highest task on the list. When it has been completed they move to the next task. This offers a clearly defined structure for work-based activities.

      ### The importance of reviewing time management
      By reviewing a diary or time log, an employee can assess whether they were being **efficient** and **effective**. They can decide whether things need to change, and may discuss their observations with their team leader, a supervisor or a manager. They should aim to **learn** from this process and make **appropriate changes** to future plans. 

      At all times, it is important that individuals keep others informed about their progress. This is because their work may have a direct impact on co-workers, other departments and, ultimately, customers. If they are having difficulty hitting deadlines, the **sooner** they can inform managers, the quicker actions can be taken to prevent delays.
