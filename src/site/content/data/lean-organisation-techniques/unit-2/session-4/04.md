    title: Team writing
    layout: media
    theme: t-bg-primary
    copy: |
      Team writing ensures that comprehensive knowledge (acquired from different perspectives) is applied to the SOP. It also creates ‘buy-in’, which increases the likelihood that SOPs will be implemented under the guidance of the writers. Finally, it trains coaches – the people who write the SOP – and encourages employees to follow the SOP because they know the coaches invested time and effort on their behalf. 

      Teams can write or edit parts of the SOP independently and then the contributions can be brought together. The draft SOP can then be circulated for further comments. Throughout the writing process, the team should be mindful of the health and safety implications of what they are creating.

      The ideal time to create a SOP is **before** a new process is introduced within the organisation. However, it is possible that an organisation latterly recognises that SOPs may improve the way a process performs.

    title: Reviewing a standard operating procedure
    theme: t-bg-primary
    copy: |
      You already know that SOPs are not unchanging documents – they are flexible and used to **maintain and improve performance**.

      SOPs needs to be changed **systematically**, in a way that focuses on **performance improvement.** Everyone involved should know which version of the SOP is being used.

      The first review of a SOP should be done soon after it has been implemented. The most appropriate time is after the first ‘batch of outputs’. These outputs could be components, assembled cars, purchase orders or many other things created by the process in question. If potential early changes are highlighted, they should be considered by the relevant team. 

      If the first review, or any later reviews, require the SOP to be amended, then it should be done. The revised SOP should be dated and given an issue number. All relevant parties should be informed that the SOP has been updated and should know the nature of the change.

      There are other events or occurrences that would lead to an immediate review (and potential update), such as a health and safety infringement.

    title: Periodic reviews
    layout: media
    theme: t-bg-supporting3
    copy: |
      There are a wide range of ‘special events’ or ‘critical incidents’ that make a review necessary. However, SOPs should also be reviewed periodically.
      
      Periodic reviews may also take place every six months or annually. They encourage team members to take a fresh look at the SOP and ask whether it can be improved. Periodic reviews also provide staff with an opportunity to make suggestions that they might not otherwise make, and that others might not think of, encouraging participation and involvement.

    title: Summary
    theme: t-bg-primary i-summary
    copy: |
      A standard operating procedure is not an unchangeable document. It is a working document that is used to maintain and improve performance within an organisation. To make improvements requires changes, which should be reflected in the SOP. The changes may be inspired by ‘special events’ or they may arise out of periodic reviews. Changes must be systematic and they must be done in a way that focuses on performance improvement. Any changes should be dated and given an appropriate issue number and everyone involved should know which version of the SOP is being used.
