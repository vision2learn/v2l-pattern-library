          title: Copyright law
          text: |
            Some copyright law, including the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, gives the people who create things like music, books, video and images, the right to control how they are used.

            This means that you can’t use information someone else has created and try to pass it off as your own. This is called plagiarism.

            Although it’s fine to import data you have created, such as a chart or table, you need to be careful when using other peoples work!
          img: it2/it2_u2_s6_p2_slide1.svg
        -
          title: Acknowledging sources
          text: |
            If you do use work that has been created by another person, or you plan to use someone else’s research data or text, you should include references to the original source.  You need to ask permission to use someone else’s images, photographs. Remember that you should also acknowledge the sources of data you are using in charts and graphs.

            References could be included in small font at the bottom of each slide, or they could be listed on a single slide at the end of the presentation.
          img: it2/it2_u2_s6_p2_slide2.png

          title: Local guidelines

            Local guidelines are constraints that are provided by the environment that the presentation is produced in. For example, an organisation may provide time and style constraints on a presentation produced in-house.

For example, if you were a teacher giving a presentation to a large class or during an assembly, you may only have a certain amount of time to give the presentation. Similarly, if you were giving a presentation as part of a sales meeting, you may only have been allocated a certain amount of time in which to give it.

            If you are creating a presentation while at work, the organisation may provide a number of guidelines for you to follow. This is known as a house style. A house style sheet contains rules that you need to pay attention to when creating your presentation. It may include things like the font style and size that you should use.
          img: it2/it2_u2_s6_p2_slide5.svg

          title: Equal opportunities
          text: |
            If you are creating the presentation on behalf of an organisation you may find that your organisation already has some equal opportunities design guidelines.

            A good presentation needs to contain relevant and important information – of course – but equally important is how that information is displayed. Good design will make your presentation look more attractive, and it will also make your presentation easier to understand. 
          img: it2/it2_u2_s6_p2_slide6.svg

          title: Who should you consider?
          text: |
            People who might struggle to understand a poorly designed presentation include:

            * Older people
            * Dyslexic people
            * Visually impaired people
            * Deaf and hard of hearing people.

          img: it2/it2_u2_s6_p2_slide7.svg
        -
          title: Accessibility
          text: |
            The most important thing to consider when looking at the design of your presentation is how accessible it is for your audience. There are a number of things that you can do to make your presentation more accessible:

            * Use a clear font and larger text
            * Avoid background and text colour combinations that are difficult to read
            * Justify your text to the left (this is easier for dyslexic people to read)
            * Increase line spacing (many people with dyslexia find it easier to read text with line spacing of 1.5 or more)
            * Provide subtitles for audio/video clips.

          img: it2/it2_u2_s6_p2_slide8.svg
