Background, ethnicity, and culture can strongly influence beliefs about mental illness and shapes attitudes towards the mentally ill, let’s look at some of these factors.

 
Social status and the concept of saving ‘face’ in Asian communities is very important and a mental health issue may cause public embarrassment and could damage reputations, this may subsequently affect marriage prospects or business transactions. Therefore, those who are suffering, and their families are less likely to seek a diagnosis.

 
Most cultures discourage males from showing physical or mental weakness. Whilst western countries are now leaning more to encouraging males to seek help, and admit to needing support, many still remain resistant as this may show inadequacy especially in more male dominated cultures.

 
Not every culture trusts western medical practices, instead they prefer to use traditional methods and often don’t see mental health as medical, but spiritual involving evil spirits.

 
Some South American cultures believe in the evil eye and don’t talk about mental health issues for fear of being labeled as “locos” or crazy

 
Buddhism and Taoism advocate spiritual understanding and believe mental health problems are a result of bad deeds in previous lives.

 
Every culture has its own way of making sense of mental health and each culture has its opinion on whether mental illness is real or imagined, an illness of the mind or the body or both, who is at risk for it, what might cause it, and perhaps most importantly, the level of stigma surrounding it. 

 
Younger people in all cultures are more likely to seek help, but older people especially those from other countries and cultures, are less likely to change their behaviour and attitude. This can cause problems and divisions in families regarding seeking a diagnosis either restricting them or making them break from tradition and subsequently the family.
