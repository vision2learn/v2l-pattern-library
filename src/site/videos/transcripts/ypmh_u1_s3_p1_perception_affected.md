There has been a fundamental shift in the attitude of society to being more aware and accepting of those with mental health problems. Common mental disorders such as depression and anxiety are discussed more openly, and people are more willing to seek help if they feel affected. 

Today individuals who have been affected by childhood trauma are more likely to talk about it than in the past and society is more aware of its potential causes. High profile cases of childhood abuse have helped to highlight cases both in popular culture and sporting circles to make this all the more possible.

Innovation in digital systems, digital and social media have helped bring mental health issues to the fingertips of everyone supplying an unlimited amount of information and websites to further our knowledge and understanding of mental health in general. 

New ideas such as mindfulness, and the use of tried and tested techniques such as yoga and meditation can help make people aware of their own mental health and more readily appreciate the problems of others.

However, there is still social stigma associated with mental health. Many people with mental health problems say that social stigma and discrimination have had a negative effect on their lives. This is because society in general still has a stereotypical view on mental illness and how it affects people. 

Some people still believe those who suffer from mental ill health, could be violent or dangerous when in fact they are more likely to suffer from violence themselves. The situation is made worse when the media portray people with mental health problems as dangerous, criminal, evil, or disabled and unable to live normal, fulfilled lives.

Most aspects of mental illness and psychological well‐being are influenced by social factors such as gender, social class, race and ethnicity.

A persons cultural background can impact on attitudes to mental health on several different levels. Knowledge, attitudes and behaviours from different social and cultural groups are not the same. 

For some ethnic groups mental illness is a taboo subject, and there is little open discussion about mental health problems. 

In some communities, caring for a child or young person with a mental health problem is seen as a family responsibility. Diagnoses are something to be kept private and not openly discussed, even with immediate family and this is partly down to the need to preserve the family’s reputation and status at all costs.

Children and young people from different ethnic or cultural groups may have a higher risk of mental health problems because they may experience a greater number of stressors, such as discrimination and isolation. 

They may also feel split between two communities, and therefore feel torn between the different social and cultural attitudes. This can be more of an issue when gender is brought into the equation, with girls much more likely to be affected than boys as equality of gender is not as prevalent in all communities. 

Shame, fear and secrecy surrounds mental illness as the causes are often misunderstood. In some communities, children and young people with mental health problems are not valued, for example, marriage prospects can be damaged by perceptions of mental illness.

The culture of secrecy that surrounds mental illness can, in part, be attributed to misunderstandings and misconceptions that have grown up around all aspects of mental health problems over many years. 

However, on the whole the younger generations generally have better attitudes to mental health problems than older age groups and will speak about mental health problems more openly among themselves in all cultures.

So, in this vein, hopefully we continue to see this increased positive trend and work towards eradicating the misconceptions and stigma over time.
