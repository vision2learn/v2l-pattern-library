JOE: Children and young people with autism can be very intense and focused about favourite toys, activities and topics of conversation. They may collect things and follow repetitive actions when entering a room. Some individuals might have very narrow interests or preoccupations, like needing to know everything possible about trains. 

BETH: Sensory sensitivities can also lead to developing obsessions and rituals. Children might like to stroke people’s hair whenever they can because they enjoy the sensation, or it helps them feel calm.

Children with autism can get upset when they can’t do their obsessive behaviour, routines or rituals.

JOE: Using obsessions or special interests to help control behaviour can make the experience more pleasurable. For example, when a parent gets a young child to eat by pretending the food is a train and their mouth is a tunnel, this approach can be used with individuals with autism. 

By using their obsession or special interest in something, creatively, they can be encouraged to do something they wouldn’t want too normally. 

BETH: Finding a positive outlet for an obsessive interest, for example, if an individual has an interest in dinosaurs, they might be more inclined to go to a museum to see fossils or eat food with a link to dinosaurs on the packaging.

JOE: Also, obsessions and special interests of individuals with autism can be used as rewards and motivators to help teach them new skills and behaviour. If they complete a task or activity or experience something new, they can have more time doing what they love.
