JOE: Many children on the autism spectrum are dependent on prompts provided by adults for staying on-task, completing activities and transitioning between activities in the home, school and community environments.

But over reliance on prompting should be avoided as this can become habit-forming in itself.

BETH: Prompt dependency can be recognised when the individual is not prepared for anything, such as getting dressed and ready to start the day, and they are relying on being told what to do.

To reduce or prevent prompt dependency there should always be a plan made for fading it out and from the outset plans should be set up to implement fading once the behaviour has become more regulated.  

JOE: By reducing prompts and only using them when needed the dependency can be reduced. 

In the beginning using prompts can help with all steps of a process, but as time progresses an individual should be encouraged to organise their own checklist, getting them to try to figure things out more.

BETH: This can be done by telling them to get dressed but leaving them to choose the steps. This can then be expanded to what to do to start the day, such as ‘it’s time to get up and get ready’ and the rest is up to them following their own checklist or timetable. 

JOE: It’s a gradual process to move an individual from intense prompt dependence to self-sufficiency and as this process begins, there should be time factored in to encourage the thinking process, rather than immediately correcting the individual. 

BETH: There is no one way of breaking prompt dependency in autism, and any changes a parent or carer makes will probably reflect in the behaviour of the child or individual. However, the goal in every instance is to encourage the individual with autism to become more independent in problem solving.
