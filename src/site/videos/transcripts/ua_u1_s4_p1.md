BETH: What are the main characteristics found in individuals with Autism? let’s have a look at these in a little more detail.

JOE: ‘Communication’.     

A child with autism may use different ways to communicate, they may echo words and phrases without context, take an adult to something that they want, rather than ask for it and take what is said literally.
 
Many individuals with autism have good language skills, whilst others will speak very little, or not at all, though this does not mean they cannot communicate in other ways. Individuals with autism typically prefer communication to be clear and simple.

BETH: ‘Social Interaction’.     

Social situations are difficult for individuals with Autism. A child may prefer to play alone and won’t respond to other adults or children. 

Young people and adults typically struggle with the ‘rules’ of social engagement, such as when to speak, when to laugh and when to empathise, and it can inhibit their understanding of the ‘bigger picture’ in relationships and contexts. It may also cause problems in education and the workplace.

JOE: ‘Repetitive and Restrictive behaviour’  

Children with autism can display repetitive behaviour, including hand flapping, toe walking, spinning wheels, lining up toy cars. Restrictive behaviour may mean a child may only eat food of a certain colour, have to walk the same route or only watch one programme over and over. 

As they get older individuals with Autism may have a need for structure and will impose their own routines in order to help make sense of the world and alleviate anxiety, and these can sometimes become obsessive behaviours and rituals.

BETH: ‘Sensory sensitivity’ 

Sensory sensitivity is common to the autism spectrum, this may be ‘over sensitivity’ or ‘under-sensitivity’ to things such as light, sound, touch and heat, or certain tastes, textures or smells. Sensory differences are most noticeable when children are over-sensitive to stimuli and may show distress at loud noises or bright lights. 

A young person or adult may also have an exceptional attention to detail, they may find working in a room with certain sounds, raised voices, flickering lights or cracks in the wall will adversely affect them. They may be affected by colours and textures and the way things are laid out, even changes in peoples appearance. 

These sensitivities may lead to various coping behaviours including avoidance of certain places or people, rocking or even using self-injury as a way of calming the overload of sensations experienced.

JOE: ‘Fixation’. 

Fixation is one of the characteristics especially associated with high functioning Autism spectrum disorder and may involve subject or ideas fixation, continually discussing the same topics in conversation, obsessively playing the same song repeatedly, or reading every article written about a certain topic. 

Many other high-functioning autistic individuals have used their focus on mathematics, biology, or writing to inspire successful careers. However, they may also experience emotional sensitivity, social difficulties, linguistic 
oddities, devotion to routine and dislike of change.

BETH: Fluctuation of Skills

Some autistic people have skills and needs that ‘fluctuate’ from day to day and moment to moment. They may have over the years, learned strategies which mask their difficulties and characteristics. The result is that the person with autism can appear to be very distracted and may seem to be day-dreaming, as they are preoccupied with experiences that others cannot appreciate. 

Often autistic individuals are seen as not having the ability to play or role play, but that doesn’t mean that they lack imagination and creative talent. Some have narrow interests in a particular topic or pastime and their obsessive behaviour, means that they may focus on that, often becoming extremely knowledgeable or proficient in it. This focus on detail can enable high levels of achievement in certain fields.
