BETH: An individual with autism who is anxious and struggling to contain their own behaviour may not be able to control their anxiety and that behaviour might cause them to develop more extreme behaviours. 

JOE: Stimming is often very enjoyable can be a good way for an individual with autism to reduce stress and anxiety. However, stimming can sometimes cause self-injury, such as, head-banging or scratching.  If this behaviour becomes in some way unsafe, the individual needs to be support them to stop or modify the behaviour or reduce their reliance on it. 

BETH: Maybe their senses are being overloaded more than usual or maybe they feel pain and can’t control it. If the behaviour of the individual cannot be de-escalated by the usual strategies, then light physical guidance may be needed. 

JOE: If they are hitting themselves, gently moving their hand away may help. If they are head banging, putting a pillow or cushion between them and the hard object will help to prevent further damage to themselves and the object. Providing pain killers for physical pain if prescribed can help to remove the stress of pain and removing them to a room where they can’t easily inflict physical injury to themselves can mean there are less danger from things around them. 

BETH: If a risk of serious harm exists, then a specialist may suggest more physical restraints such as helmets, gloves and arm restraints, which can reduce the sensory experience and frequency, as well as the harm. But these should only be used in emergencies as they don’t address the cause of the behaviour just alleviate the potential injury.

JOE: When a behaviour becomes so extreme that it causes injury to an individual or others it is a great cause for concern. When an individual is engaged in an active, disruptive stage of a behaviour, such as showing aggression or anger, the focus should be on the safety of the individual and those around them.
