Let's look at each of these wastes in more detail. Transport waste occurs when movement of any material isn't part of the immediate production.

Unnecessary movement of materials, products or people which can lead to increased costs and risk of damage or loss. For example, transporting raw materials or finished products between sites over long distances or having multiple handoffs between departments. Inventory. Too much stock leads to more than just transport waste.

Lean processing is all about just in time manufacturing. If production isn't lean, raw materials may be over ordered, meaning they need to be stored. Cash flow is affected as money is being spent on stock that might not be needed for months to come. We can see here that inventory waste can be a result of inefficient processes and unbalanced production processes.

Motion. Any job that requires excess movement, such as reaching, bending, gathering tools or moving equipment is a waste. Motion waste is usually caused by ineffective layout and poor workplace organisation. We've seen in Unit 2 that there are many factors to consider when setting up an activity workplace and motion is another.

Lack of visual controls, poor processes, and documentation will also lead to motion waste. Waiting. Any waiting time in the production process is waste. For example, waiting for approval, waiting for equipment to be repaired, or waiting for materials to arrive. Whilst these might not always be unavoidable, they can be greatly reduced through strong processes, maintenance of equipment, and consistent working methods over production.

As with inventory waste, overproduction procedures have similar waste, although this happens for different reasons. When items are produced faster than needed, it's commonly known as a push system. It can be caused by poor planning or teams working at different speeds. Maybe there's new workers in one team, which has slowed them down and lack of communication.

Alternatively, the overproduction may be down to incorrectly placed automation. Overprocessing. It's important to ensure that processing is right for the job in hand, and that nothing additional to what's needed is being done. This could include producing items that are above the quality needed, writing too many or too complicated processes, and over refining processes.

In summary, any unnecessary work that results in more time and effort than is needed is considered over processing. Defects. A good quality control process should minimise defects, and it's therefore important to have checks in place throughout the production. Defects detected early in the production can stop difficulties occurring for teams further along, which can impact on overproduction, waiting, and inventory.

Where products are sent out with defects, customers will return them, which leads to further storage and time spent on customer complaints. Unutilised talent.
