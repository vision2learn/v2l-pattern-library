**Abraham:** When designers create a new system, they often start by looking at what the system needs to do and what it should look like. But when designing any system, it is always important to consider cyber security as an integral part of the design process in order to create a system that is robust.

**Nicole:** Traditionally organisations have been more reactive to cyber security, but this can be a costly process in more ways than one. By being proactive and designing systems with cyber security already built in they are limiting the opportunity for tech savvy hackers to create maximum damage.

**Abraham:** If the specifications of a system highlight cyber security as integral, then the system will be designed around that criterion. 

**Nicole:** Before a system is designed it should be looked at carefully to see what data, connections, people, and other systems may be needed for it to operate and how these need to be taken into consideration regarding security. The point at which any cyber attack could happen should be thoroughly reviewed. This should be at every point at which data is stored, manipulated, or rendered.

**Abraham:** It is also important to assess what risks cannot be accepted and what the consequences of an attack would be at every level. Companies should always do a risk analysis beforehand to assess where the potential problems might lie and what the consequences might be if cyber security is not implemented from the beginning.

**Nicole:** Everyone involved in the design process should also consider the cost of not doing something versus the cost of doing it. 

So, what would happen when a system stops functioning due to a cyber-attack. It can greatly impact both users of the system and the organisation who owns and manages the system. 

**Abraham:** Unauthorised access may mean that the system has been modified or had its data destroyed, meaning that if a ‘backup’ is not available all that data may be lost.

Also, all that data accessed, could be sold on, or given to others to use. 

**Nicole:** You only have to look at the impact this has had on companies and organisations such as Eurofins scientific who were infected by ransomware and the NHS which was brought to a standstill by the WannaCry outbreak. For more information on this check out our weblinks.

**Abraham:** Costs don’t just involve repairing or patching a system or payment of ‘ransoms’ to get a system back online or retrieve data. They involved much greater costs such as being fined under GDPR or NIS legislation as well as general business costs, and major reputational damage let alone the chance of litigation. There are also issues related to loss of trust amongst customers, users or suppliers and an inability to meet contractual obligations.

**Nicole:** So, if the worst happens and a system is found vulnerable, what are the consequences? If data is imported from an external or less trusted source it may have been designed to attack a system. Employees at all levels should be made aware of the consequences of a data breach and their own responsibility as part of the organisation. 

**Abraham:** It is always important that structure data can be validated or else it should be transformed, or it needs to be rendered carefully. The complexity of malicious code in documents means that it is safer to transform the file or content into another format. 

**Nicole:** Checking that the structure and content of data is as expected goes some way to ensuring that malicious code will not be injected into a system, but this only works in simple file formats. If validation and transformation are not possible rendering information in a disposable environment not linked to the system is the best option.

**Abraham:** However, as we said earlier, by adding security at the beginning in the design stage all these issues can be factored into the process to make a system as secure as possible.
