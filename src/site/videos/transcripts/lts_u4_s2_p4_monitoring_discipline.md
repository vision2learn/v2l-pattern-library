In this video we are going to look at monitoring discipline and how can we identify the interpersonal behaviour or support the skills required by a manger.

Your organisation should have policies in place to guide you with resolving disciplinary issues, so it is important that you take time to familiarise yourself with them.

Disciplinary issues at work are usually to do with an employee’s conduct or with their capability, or ability to do their work to the right standard.

So what behaviours and skills do you need?

There are interpersonal skills which can be developed to help you monitor the discipline in your team and in general it’s best to try and resolve disciplinary issues informally at first, if appropriate Having these skills can often help avoid having to use formal mechanisms wherever possible, but only when not deemed necessary.

Coaching and mentoring are great skills and behaviours to have when it comes to capability issues. 

Coaching involves guiding someone to help them achieve their goals and potential and mentoring involves sharing your knowledge, skills, and experience with someone so they can develop their own skillset. These are useful for dealing with issues of capability, when the first action should be to help them in achieving the expected standard and not punish them for failing. 

Coaching and mentoring are powerful tools in enabling personal professional development and career development. Personal professional development involves assessing goals and aims, and the skills and abilities needed to achieve them, and this covers general life goals as well as career goals. 

Career development is similar, though specifically focused on career goals. 

Having the ability to coach and mentor your team will not only help them through capability issues but will also empower their personal professional and career development.

So how do we approach difficult conversations?

When talking to people about conduct and capability it can be tricky as it touches on sensitive subjects. Being able to have difficult conversations is an important skill for team leaders and managers, and there’s different methods for doing this. 

You should be calm and empathetic during the conversation, making sure you listen when the other person expresses their feelings and point of view about the situation. Be prepared by writing a list of key points you want to cover, to stay on topic, also stick to the facts as much as possible, rather than being drawn into “She said/ He said” debates. Keep the atmosphere positive and focussed on fixing the situation, rather than apportioning blame. 

Provide clear feedback is often related to difficult conversations but is also a skill, in its own right. When giving someone feedback about their conduct or performance, that feedback should be precise and not open to misinterpretation. Try to stick to the facts as much as possible, even using data if its available. Also think about what’s the best way to give someone feedback if it’s about something sensitive. Better to do it in person rather than by email.

Organisational skills

As a team leader, you’re responsible for making sure your team stays organised and efficient. Workplace conflicts that lead to disciplinary issues are often caused by teams being poorly organised. By taking steps to make sure your team is well organised; everyone has a clearly defined job role, there are strong lines of communication, and people understand the goals they’re expected to achieve; your team will be much less prone to disciplinary issues. 

Spend time each day thinking about how your team are going to achieve their tasks. What problems can you anticipate? What’s gone right or wrong before? Doing small things like making ‘To Do lists’ is a great way of staying better organised and the more organised you are, the better the example you’re setting for your team.

Empathy

Disciplinary issues often have an emotional component. The standard of someone’s work may have declined because of emotional trouble or their conduct may be inappropriate because of other frustrations elsewhere in their life. 

When responding to disciplinary issues, try putting yourself in the other person’s shoes. Showing understanding of what it feels like to be the other person and their situation can help you gauge the right response and help to figure out why it’s happening. 

Being more empathetic and understanding the feeling of others can make you a more observant and approachable team leader. This also enables your team to feel you are more approachable and can help make disciplinary issues less likely to occur and escalate. 

Safeguarding

Disciplinary issues can also be due to complicated causes that are not under the person’s control. These may be inside or outside of work. By investigating what’s going on, you might find that there are safeguarding issues. 

These can occur when someone is judged to be vulnerable due to age, disability, illness, or an inability to protect themselves from abuse or communicate abuse appears to be or actually is suffering abuse or neglect. If there are members of your team who are considered vulnerable adults, you should be especially mindful of their status, if there are disciplinary issues involving them, as this could be a sign of safeguarding issues. 

You should immediately escalate this to HR and to law enforcement or other external bodies, if necessary, especially if there is reason to believe there is physical or sexual abuse. You can be prosecuted for failing in your safeguarding duties, so it is important to treat this very seriously.
