JOE: Let’s look in the process of an Autism Assessment. When an individual attends their assessment, the assessor will discuss any problems and the interaction of the person will be watched to look for potential signs. 

There may need to be more appointments to follow, and those who know the individual well, will be asked for any relevant information they can provide.

BETH: When the individual is a child the school, nursery or GP may be asked to fill in a report. For a young person or adult, they may be asked to fill in a questionnaire about themselves with the GP supplying any other medical information. 

At the end of the process a report will be produced, detailing whether the individual meets the criteria for Autism spectrum diagnosis, and it will give information about what help may be needed with communication, social interaction and behaviours or sensitivity and indicate any strengths.

JOE: If a formal assessment has been done but it does not clearly show whether or not they are on the spectrum, or if someone disagrees with the diagnosis, they can be referred again or maybe have a private assessment. 

Some individuals don’t want to go through the process of a formal diagnosis and may prefer to try an online autism test, but these cannot guarantee accuracy. 

BETH: Informal assessments of individuals with autism spectrum disorders involve the collection of information about the individual, interpreting that information so as to better understand the individual’s abilities. Informal assessment is usually about gaining information in order to set goals, identify strategies, and measure outcomes.

Although the overall goals of informal and formal assessment are similar, they differ in the manner, type and use, of the information collected.

JOE: In the past formal assessment was broken down into different diagnoses such as Aspergers syndrome, Autistic disorder, Kanner’s syndrome, childhood autism, atypical autism and pervasive developmental disorder not otherwise specified (PDD-NOS). These all now come under the term Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) or Autism for short, for all diagnoses.

BETH: The emphasis now on diagnosis is the specific ‘levels' of Autism spectrum disorder, allowing for more clarity to be placed on where they fit on the spectrum. The levels range from mild to severe symptoms. 

JOE: There are three levels of autism: 
- Level 1 requiring some support; 
- Level 2 requiring substantial support 
- Level 3 requiring very substantial support

These levels are based on a person's strengths and limitations in regard to their ability to communicate, adapt to new situations, expand beyond restricted interests, and manage daily life. 

BETH: Although the levels of support are useful for indicating where an autistic person falls on the spectrum regarding severity, they have their limitations. 

They can be very subjective, for example, some may need support at school but are fine at home, while others may do well at school but struggle in social situations. 

It is therefore up to the clinicians to identify the individual support needed for each person that is diagnosed.
