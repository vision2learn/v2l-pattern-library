JOE: All children reach a range of developmental milestones between birth and adulthood. Children with autism, however, may reach some on time, or even early, but lose ground later or not reach some milestones at all. Some may develop skills that are very advanced, but not the useful ones they need in everyday life.

BETH: Here are, a few developmental markers that are most likely and most obvious when a child has autism spectrum disorder. Problems such as working simple toys, not speaking in sentences, not understanding simple instructions, not playing as others and the lack of eye contact.

JOE: The physical changes of puberty can have an alarming impact on individuals with autism. With girls, their breasts develop, and the menstruation starts, with boys their voice gets lower, and the penis grows larger. 

BETH: Girls may struggle with the concept of periods but should be shown that it is a natural process and not to be concerned, and they should be shown how to use feminine hygiene products. Boys should equally be told about nighttime ejaculation and erections so as to allay their fears. 

JOE: Both should be instructed through personal hygiene routines and why this is more important as they progress through puberty to become an adult. These could be done by means of visual supports such as pictures and diagrams.

During puberty, most individuals start to experience sexual urges and It’s normal for children on the spectrum to feel sexually aroused. 

BETH: For teens with autism associated sensory issues, these new sensations can cause anxiety as they don’t know how to handle these new sensations. Masturbation is a healthy and normal part of development, however, some teens on the autism spectrum lack the social awareness skills to know when and where it’s not appropriate.
