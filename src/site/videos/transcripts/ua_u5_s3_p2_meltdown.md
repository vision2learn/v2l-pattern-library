JOE: Many autistic people will show signs of distress before having a meltdown, this is sometimes called the “rumble stage”. They may start to exhibit signs of anxiety like pacing or pacing, rocking or becoming very still.  At this stage, there may still be a chance to prevent a meltdown happening, by using distraction, diversion and calming strategies and removing potential triggers.

BETH: It can help to identify the cause of the potential meltdown as it is a reaction to an overwhelming experience. Recording what is happening before, during and after each meltdown may show patterns in its occurrence like particular times, particular places, or when something particular has happened.
 
Once a clearer idea of what may be triggering meltdowns is more evident, ways to minimise the triggers can be organised. 

JOE: Every individual with autism is different, but sensory differences, changes in routine, anxiety, and communication difficulties are common triggers.

A meltdown happens when an individual becomes completely overwhelmed by their current situation and temporarily loses control of their behaviour. This can be expressed verbally by shouting, screaming or crying. It can also be expressed physically by kicking, lashing out and biting, or it could be expressed in both ways.

BETH: It is not bad or naughty behaviour, like a temper tantrum, as the individual is completely overwhelmed. Their disorder means that it is difficult to express their feelings in any other way and the result is a meltdown. 

JOE: Meltdowns are not the only way however, that individuals may express feeling overwhelmed. They may withdraw from situations and refuse to interact.

If someone is having a meltdown, or not responding, they should be given some time to recover from information or sensory overload. If they are in a public place, they should be asked calmly if they are OK and given time to respond. Others should be kept away so as not to cause more distress. Sensory stimuli and information overload should be reduced wherever possible by taking them quietly somewhere calmer and safer. Strategies such as deep breathing could also be used.

BETH: When they are in full meltdown mode, they may lack the ability to be reasoned with, redirected or distracted. However, this level of agitation does not just happen, it can be anticipated and de-escalated. 

It is important then to have a plan for what to do in a crisis. Details of events, triggers or signs that a crisis situation might develop, should be documented along with tools and strategies for keeping the individual and those around them safe. 

JOE: Intervention steps and de-escalation procedures related to each increasing level of agitation, should be noted and lists of things to do and not to do according to the fears and needs of the individual should be clearly shown. Previous information from past episodes of concern should be clearly indicated along with information and contact details of family, professionals or support who need to be contacted in the event of a problem.
