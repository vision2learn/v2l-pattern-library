**Leah:** So, what do we mean when we talk about security by design?

Security has often long been considered an afterthought in the software development process, with measures to address potential issues, typically tacked on just before release. This can cause serious issues as components are so interdependent and changing a single piece of code could affect the whole application adversely.

**Chris:** The focus previously has on implementing security controls around physical infrastructure such as networks, servers and endpoints using firewalls, but this approach, is no longer adequate in sustaining today's expectations for rapid and reliable deployment of software.

**Leah:** In software development you will hear all about the ‘software development lifecycle’, but you will also encounter the ‘security by design lifecycle’. Both of these terms are closely linked as the starting point for both is the initial idea, which is then taken through the stages of design, develop, test, deliver and support. 

**Chris:** The difference between the two is that ‘Security by design’ focuses on the requirements relating to relevant security features such as data integrity, authentication, confidentiality, authorisation and privacy.

**Leah:** Taking ‘security by design’ into consideration at the very beginning of a project helps to reduce costs and reduce the potential risks that may be faced when the software is released. 

Building security into the development process and testing cycles, is good practice and can save on costs, extra time and reliability. This will in turn also help to maintain client confidence in their product.

**Chris:** ‘Security by design’ is about having a risk-based approach to development that focuses on continuous assessment, analysis, improvement, and validation to create safer practices and better safety nets. 

**Leah:** An analogy that is often used in security is the ‘Castle and Moat model’ that means everyone inside the castle is protected. Think of the moat and the drawbridge as high-quality code that create that security and the tack on elements are like the foot soldiers. If the castle walls and the moat and drawbridge are in good order, then the foot soldiers will only be needed in emergencies or in case of a breach. But failure to create a secure structure in the first place and maintain it well, will result in cracks appearing which even though they can be filled, will eventually give way!

**Chris:** Failure to incorporate security by design will continue the cycle of further development, patches and bug fixes as and when required which takes up further time, resources and for those who are using the software a potential lack of confidence in the software itself.
