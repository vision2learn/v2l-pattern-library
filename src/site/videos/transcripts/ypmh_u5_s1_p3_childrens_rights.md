RHIANNON:  
Children’s human rights are the basic things, that children need to thrive, they need to be free and live with dignity and be protected from violence or abuse. They should have an adequate standard of living, the right to education, to play, be healthy, and be cared for. 

 
LEAH:  
Children need to have their own rights over and above ‘human rights’ related to adults, because they are separate and unique to adults, and need special protection because of their vulnerability and developing maturity. They are often less articulate than adults and have special developmental needs and evolving capacities and are less likely to be taken seriously.

 
RHIANNON:  
Once a child or young person develops mental health concerns those human rights can become more of an issue, but they are no different.

 
Children and young people are entitled to respect for their privacy and confidentiality, for example, in getting advice and counselling on mental health matters, depending on their age and understanding.

 
LEAH:  
Many of the aims of services helping children and young people who are experiencing mental health, emotional and psychological issues, align with human rights values. These include supporting young people to be involved in their care and treatment plans.

 
Children and young people should be treated with dignity and respect in their interactions with services, and it should be ensured that family members or carers are able to participate in discussions.

 
RHIANNON:  
All children with mental health issues need to be protected from violence, abuse, stigma and mistreatment in institutions such as health and social care facilities, schools and the criminal justice system.

 
Any ‘seclusion’ or ‘isolation’ of a young person against their will or without parental consent, especially where the young person lacks competence or capacity to consent, could lead to potential human rights issues.

 
LEAH:  
Seclusion or isolation should only really be used as a last resort, and not as a punishment or way to encourage good behaviour, as this could have a serious impact on the young person.

 
Children’s best interests should always be central, taking action to enable all children and young people to have the best outcomes.
