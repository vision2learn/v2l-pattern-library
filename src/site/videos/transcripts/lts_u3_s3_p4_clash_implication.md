It’s inevitable that sometimes an organisation and the people in it, will have different values and attitudes. When this happens, it can be difficult to find the best solution for everyone, after all everyone in the organisation is expected to observe the organisation’s values.

As a team leader, you might find yourself in this situation and that your loyalties are conflicted. On one hand, it is important to keep the organisation united and effective, but on the other hand, people in the organisation have the right to express their own opinions, including disagreements with organisational values.

If you tell your team that you won’t tolerate disagreement with the organisational values, that won’t encourage them to think freely and innovatively. But conversely, if you allow people to disregard what the organisation expects from them, this will damage the work you need to do and your team’s cohesion. 

When a clash emerges, people pull in different directions. Whatever the clash is about, the fact that you’ve lost that unity, means that things are going to start going wrong. You no longer have a single clear pathway to achieve your goals, and people will start doing things in different ways, causing confusion, which can have ‘knock on’ effects. 

Targets may be missed and resentments between people can grow.

So, let’s have a look at an example of this:

Suppose a new person starts in sales. They have a great track record and lots of experience and they really want to make a good first impression. The company really values honesty and building long-term relationships, having a small number of loyal clients they can rely on rather than the uncertainty of a working with a lot of unknown clients on small projects.

The new person, however, has different values, they believe in getting as big a roster of clients as possible, even if that means stretching the truth about what can be delivered. Although they would never tell an outright lie, they believe that being a bit economical with the truth and making the occasional exaggeration, is just good sales strategy.

What we see here is a conflict in individual values and organisational expectations, and this could cause resentment from other members of the sales team if they perceive the new person to be breaking the rules they follow. This could also cause big problems with production if they introduce far more clients than the organisation is set up to expect. In a word, this could cause problems, which in turn could cause more problems…

This shows a clash in values. The new person’s values, focus on bringing in as many clients as possible, while the organisation’s, are focused on building strong relationships with a small number of clients, and these are incompatible. 

As a team leader, in a situation like this, you’d need to make sure the organisation’s values are being clearly communicated to everyone in the team from the beginning. You might want to consider sending out emails explaining what the values are and why they matter, putting posters up about the values, or giving presentations about them. This will help to keep people on track and stop conflicts from happening in the first place.

Ignoring the problem won’t help resolve it. Instead, you should start by talking to the people involved, and encouraging them to express what their values are whilst reminding them what the organisation expects from them. You should also make sure you remind them why these values exist, and what they’re intended to achieve. This will help them understand that the values aren’t arbitrary.

You should also look to see if you can find some common ground, and if there are principles, they may be willing to adapt. It’s always best to try and find a compromise if you can, but not at the expense of the organisational values. 

You may even end up having to find a different role for the person in the organisation, where their values don’t cause conflict. If we consider our example, in this case maybe the new person in sales, would be better suited to working in marketing. 

And if all else fails and the problem doesn’t go away, you may have no other option than to formally escalate it as a disciplinary matter, but always make sure you follow your organisation’s policies for doing this.
