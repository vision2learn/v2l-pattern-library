**Chris:** When planning any project, it’s essential that all the information required is captured and documented well in advance of the projects start date. This is the starting point for the projects development as it can be used to keep all stakeholders aligned to the achievement of a single goal. Creating detailed business requirement documentation can also assist with budgets and time frames for the project. 

**Leah:** To ensure that the business fully understands the needs of the project and are able to define objectives that are attainable, a range of steps should be taken during the requirements gathering process to help define how to achieve the project’s objective. 

Defining the business requirements can be complex as needed to consider the tasks and goals of the business as well as the client’s needs.

**Chris:** The first step would be to understand who all the stakeholders are and what their requirements are. 

**Leah:** Stakeholders are anyone involved in or affected by the project. In any project there will be multiple stakeholders which may include end users, shareholders, developers, system administrators, customers, marketing teams, sales teams, clients and relevant regulatory authorities. There will also be a ‘project manager’ and ‘product owner’ who will analyse this information and work with the various teams and individuals to develop the project plan.

**Chris:** You then need to think about the types of requirements needed, most of these can be defined as functional or non-functional. 

Functional requirements are usually set out in detailed ‘Functional Requirements Documentation’ [FRD], and a ‘Statement of Work’ [SOW] gives an overview of the high-level objectives and requirements of the product. 

Non-functional requirements cover anything that is not relevant to a product’s functionality such as performance, stability, security, and technical specifications. 

**Leah:** For example, ‘Functional requirements’ for a website may be the ability to process payments, whilst a ‘non-functional requirement’ would be an ‘SSL certificate’ to ensure that payments that are processed are secure. 

There may also be other requirements such as legal, regulatory or even additional training for those involved in the development of the project. 

**Chris:** To make sure the information within the requirements documents is comprehensive and covers the needs of all stakeholders, it needs to be gathered correctly, this is known as ‘elicitation activities’ These may include document analysis, brainstorming sessions, surveys, focus groups, prototyping, observations and requirement workshops. 

**Leah:** Businesses my use one or more different methods to achieve the level of detail required. The amount of information for a single user client may only involve having meetings, whereas developing software for wider use will require a much wider range of elicitation methods. 

**Chris:** During the information gathering stage it is critical to record the results of all elicitation activities, such as a meeting or a brainstorming session. If not over time information may become unclear or confused. This can be done by means of a simple word document, presentation or spreadsheet, to sophisticated requirement managements systems and modelling diagrams. Initial brainstorming sessions or meetings with stakeholders could be done on a whiteboard, encouraging collaboration and creativity. 

**Leah:** Once the requirements information is recorded it needs to be reviewed and analysed, to make sure everything is clear and concise with no ambiguity. If needed revisions can be made before being ready to be finalise and implement.

**Chris:** The final documentation for the project will be produced, most likely by a technical writer who will provide a Statement of Work [SOW], Functional Requirements Document [FRD] and Business Requirement Documentation. There will also be a range of plain language documentation produced. 

**Leah:** Once the project starts there may need to be changes to the requirements and the ‘requirements documentation’ should show this as the project evolves. It is vital these changes or new developments are incorporated, and stakeholders informed, otherwise the documentation will no longer be fit for purpose and the project could face serious issues or delays. 

**Chris:** When working in an Agile environment, any changes will be added to the backlog by the product owner, and those involved in the project will carry out backlog refinement at a dedicated meeting during the current sprint. Here the product owner and the team will discuss the existing priorities as well as any items that have been added, carry out refinement, and then prioritise the items accordingly
