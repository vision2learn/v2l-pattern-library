JOE: The ‘Autism act’ was the first legislation to specifically focus on Autism, even though the other acts do support individuals in different ways. 

Because of the Act, most councils now have a diagnosis pathway for adults, a specific ‘Autism Lead’ and make sure that every autistic person has the right to a social care assessment, something which was difficult for many autistic people in the past.

BETH: The ‘Fulfilling and Rewarding Lives’ strategy aimed to improve physical and mental health, reduce crime and substance misuse, reduce dependency on benefits and raise awareness amongst professionals, also to make sure diagnostic services are available and ensure that transitions into adult services are managed successfully.  

JOE: ‘Think autism’ further built on this by improving all aspects and helping those with autism get into work, developing skills and independence. 

These strategies outline some of the areas that needed to be improved, such as autism awareness training, access to a diagnosis and assessment and local service and support.

BETH: Importantly, the strategies and guidance state that a diagnosis of any autistic spectrum condition, including Asperger syndrome or high-functioning autism, is a reason to assess somebody for support and though it does not guarantee services will be provided, assessment should be carried out and look at the person rather than their diagnosis.

JOE: There is emphasis on aiming to improve public services for people with autism, such as transport, leisure, employment and the police, to get better at adjusting to, and meeting the needs of, people with autism.

Local authorities need to keep and provide data about autism in their area, such as the numbers of autistic people known about, the range of need for the support for them to live independently and the age profile of people with autism.

BETH: While progress has been made since the Autism Act, there is still much to be done in society to show that autistic people have the same rights to life and independence as other people. 

If these strategies are successful and can change how society views autism, this can lead to real improvements in the day-to-day lives of autistic people, as well as carers and professionals that support them.
