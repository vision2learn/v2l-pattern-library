**Nicole:** OSINT or open-source intelligence is information that comes from publicly available sources, that can be accessed by the use of internet resources. These sources can be from television, newspaper articles, research papers and industry journals, census and public record data, business and trade documents, blogs and social media posts, websites and more. 

**Abraham:** Some of this data can be found just by a simple google search, some search for much harder to find information, that is not found on normal search engines, and these can include database, image, and deep and dark web searches. **Nicole:** When cyber security professionals search for ‘open-source intelligence’ they are usually searching for specific sources of information, and often use advanced search functions, specialist software tools and websites.

Collecting and analysing data gathered from these open sources can produce actionable intelligence. It can be used for national security, law enforcement, business intelligence and is of value to a wide range of cyber security professionals, such as intelligence analysts.

**Abraham:** OSINT can be used for many different things including ‘Counter Terrorism’, ‘Violent Extremist Threat Monitoring’, ‘Security Vetting’, ‘fraud and financial crime detection’, ‘organised crime and trafficking detection’ and corporate security.

So why is it important to only use reliable and valid sources of Open Source Intelligence information?

**Nicole:** With so much Open Source Intelligence information available, cyber security professionals need to make sure that their sources of information are both reliable and valid. Accessing and using unreliable sources and inaccurate data can mean they may be wasting time in searching through information that is invalid, out of date or totally wrong. It may even involve chasing false leads and missing relevant vulnerabilities and threats. 

**Abraham:** Inaccurate sources of information can also cause serious problems for organisations and sometimes for other people. Imagine someone being falsely identified or falsely accused. This can lead to serious legal issues, including large fines, for breaches of the GDPR laws. 

**Nicole:** Although OSINT is readily and publicly available, the use of it and its storage and processing is subject to GDPR or General Data Protection Regulation, and any business or organisation using it must adhere to the GDPR rules and regulations.

This includes safeguarding that information from cyber attack and having permission to process any personal data.

So, what happens if in general reliable sources of information are not used?

**Nicole:** As we all know technology is constantly changing and new threats are also constantly emerging. To make sure the intelligence about those threats is reliable the source of the information also needs to be reliable. Unreliable sources can often lead to false information in the form of false positives and false negatives.

**Abraham:** What are false positives? False positives are when a threat is seen to be there, but it isn’t real. Investigating this can involve cyber security teams in wasting many hours and resources and may also distract from seeing and following up a real threat.

**Nicole:** What are false negatives? False negatives lead cyber security professionals to believe there is no threat, when there is, or overlook a threat that that may not seem dangerous. This could reduce the ability of the security team to respond to or minimise an attack when it happens as they are not prepared. A false negative could also be part of hiding a more sophisticated attack which is capable of evading early detection therefore making it less preventable.
