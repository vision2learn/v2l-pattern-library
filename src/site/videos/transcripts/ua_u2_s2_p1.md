JOE: When assessing and planning for the care of a child or young person with autism, they and their parents should be able to express their views, wishes and feelings. They should also be a part of the decision-making process which should always be made clear and easy to understand.

The child or young person, and those that know them best, should be able to talk about what they have done, what they are interested in, highlight their strengths and capabilities and what outcomes they are seeking in the future.

BETH: When a child or young person with Autism is transitioning into Adult services and support, all teams should work jointly to provide support, further assessment and services that make that transition positive. Positivity is always important to help the individual make better choices, try new things or learn new skills.

Diagnosis and management should be reviewed throughout the transition process, and there should be clarity about who is the lead clinician to ensure continuity of care and maintain that ‘person centered’ support.

JOE: If the individual is an adult, families, partners and carers should also have the opportunity to be involved in decisions about support and care. 

Good communication between healthcare professionals and people with autism and their families, partners and carers, is essential and time should be taken to build a trusting, supportive, empathic and non-judgemental relationship.

BETH: This should enable support to be structured to create an ‘outcome focused’, co-ordinated plan, that meets the needs of the individual. It should be supported by evidence-based written information tailored to the person's needs.

It should also be accessible to people with additional needs, such as physical, sensory or learning disabilities, and to people who do not speak or read English, however if individuals do not have the mental capacity, the code of practice that accompanies the Mental Capacity act should always be followed.

JOE: *Remember an important thing to focus on with a person-centred approach is not to see the ‘limitations’ someone may have, but to see them as differences and as something the individual can overcome with support. 

Showing empathy and compassion and being supportive, can help to gain understanding of the reactions and behaviours someone with autism may experience.

BETH: By being non judgemental and not blaming the person helps us to understand their needs and appreciate any improvements to their social skills and interactions with others.

Treating someone as an individual, can make them feel more comfortable and supported, allowing them to open up and take on new challenges.
