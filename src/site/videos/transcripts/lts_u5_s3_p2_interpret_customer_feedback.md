In this video we are going to discuss how we can use customer feedback to help improve an organisation.

Customer feedback isn’t especially useful when it’s just taken on its own. For it to make sense and help your organisation, you need to learn to be able to interpret it and analyse it. When you start making real sense of customer feedback, you’ll have a powerful tool for continual organisation and product improvement. So, it’s important that you get this right.

It’s best to start by grouping the feedback into different categories, such as customer service experience, website experience, and product satisfaction. By grouping feedback like this, it is easier to see the patterns that will start to emerge, such as does one category of feedback seem to attract more negative comments than positive? 

You might also notice that customers are happy with the product and their interactions with customer service assistants, but are not happy about the website experience. This can help you as you might have assumed that customers really liked the website because you personally approved it, but the feedback is telling you a different story and the website is not as successful as the product or service.

It’s also important to always be objective when looking at feedback, so try to stick to the facts and figures, rather than just speculating without any evidence, or just basing things on your opinions or of those around you. The facts revealed by interpreting the feedback are often a much more reliable guide when it comes to improving how to do things or making changes for the better.

Try looking for common themes or repeated statements in the feedback you’re receiving. Remember if more than one person has had the same issue, or negative experience then this is a problem to be addressed, so you can find out what’s going on. Or maybe your attention is drawn to positive experiences people have had, that hadn’t been recognised before, such as finding out about a new, important skill that someone has, or an opportunity for development and improvement is identified. 

The volume of feedback can also be an important piece of information in its own right. If you start receiving more feedback across all the categories, either negative or positive, this maybe an indication that this needs your attention. 

Ask yourself, has there been a big change in how the team works? 

Was there a new marketing promotion? 

Has your core product changed? 

Likewise, if the amount of feedback starts to reduce in any or all areas, you should investigate what might have caused that, and whether or not the remaining feedback is positive or negative. 

Sometimes the most important feedback to address won’t be noticeable by its amount but will be more significant due to its strategic importance. Imagine if one or two users discover that there’s a dramatically unsafe feature in your main product. Even though only a small number of people may have noticed this, it may mean that everyone with that product might now be at risk, without even realising it. This sort of feedback should be investigated swiftly and often will involve a recall.

Finally, feedback doesn’t do anything to help you on its own, even once you’ve interpreted it. It is the actual concrete steps that you take, to act on the feedback that you receive, that changes things and the best way to do this, is to form action plans with your team and other key stakeholders. 

By structuring the action plan based on objective facts derived from customer feedback, you’re putting the customers’ insights to good use and improving your organisation and future customer experiences.
