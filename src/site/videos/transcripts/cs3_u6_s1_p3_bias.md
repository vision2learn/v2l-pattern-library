**Nicole:** So, what exactly is bias? Bias is the inclination or prejudice for or against something or someone. We all have biases that often impact our decisions, and this also goes for cyber security. 

**Abraham:** Cyber security personnel have to address what alerts to investigate, what systems to patch and often these decisions are affected by biases which help us to react more quickly.

**Nicole:** Imagine if the current threat landscape shows a high level of breaches by foreign hackers, then cyber security teams might be on the lookout for these and may miss the more common internal or localised social engineering threat.

**Abraham:** Threat actors use this bias to try to exploit and threaten our security.

By being aware of bias and how it can affect our decision making, it can help us to avoid making mistakes.

**Nicole:** Let’s have a look at some of the different types of bias and how they may affect cyber security issues:

Cognitive Bias is the result of the brains attempt to simplify information processing; we create our own subjective reality due to our perception of the input. We may see or hear something but perceive the information differently to someone , even though the information we both receive is the same. 

**Abraham:** It is therefore important to discuss things with others to see their point of view on the situation.

Availability Bias is a mental shortcut based on past examples relating to the available information. If we are used to hearing this information, then we recall it quickly and therefore it can influence our ‘go to’ solution and as such, if a problem was in a specific area previously, we might automatically feel that it is the same again, rather than looking at the bigger picture.

**Nicole:** Availability bias is also evident when an organisation prepares for ‘high impact’ ‘low probability events’ instead of ‘high probability’ events. High probability events should be mostly prepared for as they are more likely to happen!

**Abraham:** Confirmation Bias is when more attention is paid to things that confirm your own beliefs rather than looking for other points of view. 

**Nicole:** Imagine investigating a data breach by a suspected malicious employee, then that is the evidence you would be looking for and subsequently you might miss the real cause.

**Abraham:** Implicit or Unconscious Bias can influence our bias by believing in the experts especially if you already have an association with them. If they think it’s right, then it must be, without even challenging the data and looking at alternative views. 

**Nicole:** Being on the side of the majority is not always the best approach. You should look at the facts and then make your own decisions and if they differ from others identify, then look at why this is. 

**Abraham:** In cyber security there may often be assumptions made about users due to bias. This may involve an age approach which could follows the adage ‘older and wiser’ or conversely older therefore ‘not up to date’. 

**Nicole:** Younger people can also be seen as more knowledgeable of new technology, but may be reckless and likely to get involved in risky behaviour. Both ends of the spectrum can be right for various reasons but how we perceive things is more often than not, related to our bias.

**Abraham:** Cyber security professionals need to always investigate with an open mind and not automatically think that the user is to blame. To tackle this bias the cyber security professional should go beyond the user and see if the system is to blame, ultimately making it much easier for accidents to happen after all no one is perfect.
