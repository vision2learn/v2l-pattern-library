**Abraham:** Let's have a look at some of the core terminology used in cyber security, starting with Software. 

**Nicole:** We all know that software consists of instructions that tell a computer system what to do. It is everything to do with the computer that isn’t hardware, [the physical components of a system.] Software can be a set of programs compiled into a package such as Internet browser, operating system and general programs used to run a computer, as well as complex systems used for companies and organisations. Without the software the hardware wouldn’t run. 

**Abraham:** However, software can also attack a computer and its systems in the form of Malware. This is an umbrella term that describes all forms of malicious software including viruses, trojans, worms and ransomware. It can be used to steal information or damage a system for malicious reasons or just for financial gain. Anti-malware is key to keeping systems secure against attack.

**Nicole:** DoS and DDoS attacks involve the ‘denial of service’ to the owner of the system. In a DoS attack a computer is used to flood or crash a system with too much internet traffic causing it to slow down and eventually stop. This can be managed by using firewalls at key points in a network [multiple computers and devices connected together]. Firewalls are network security devices that monitor, and filter incoming and outgoing network traffic based on previously established security policies. They are also as simple as a barrier, that sits between a home computer and the internet.

**Abraham:** A DDoS ‘distributed denial of service’ attack involves multiple systems orchestrating a synchronised attack by Botnets [interconnected devices running software] directing malicious network traffic or data to a single target, overloading a server. These attacks can be seriously disruptive!

**Nicole:** Cloud technology allows access to files and services from anywhere in the world through the internet. It consists of a collection of high-capacity storage computers that remotely work as servers. Cloud Storage is very popular for the safe storage of files due to its secure accessibility anytime through Cloud services, the infra- structure and software hosted by a third-party provider. Cloud services facilitate the flow of data from the user via the internet or VPN [virtual private network] to their systems and back again. This can offer users, all the facilities, without all the initial financial outlay, for a fee depending on how many services they use.

**Abraham:** All data needs to be stored and whether this is in the cloud, in storage media and/or in transit it needs to be ‘encrypted’ to be secure. Encryption is a form of cryptography where a message is encoded so that only those who should see it can access it by means of a ‘key’. Encryption keys consist of random strings of unintelligible numbers, letters, and symbols. This is done to prevent theft by hackers.

**Nicole:** BYOD stands for Bring Your Own Device and involves employees who use their own devices for work. With working from home and general remote working becoming more common, many individuals have their own devices that they use for business, as well as in a personal capacity. A BYOD policy sets any limitations and restrictions on whether or not a personal device can be connected over the corporate network, depending on the security systems in place.

**Abraham:** We are always hearing about hackers and how bad they are, but sometimes hacking can be used for good. A PenTest or Penetration test is also classed as ‘ethical hacking’ and is an authorised simulated cyber attack, on a computer system using ‘hackers tools’, to assess whether there are any exploitable vulnerabilities. This is different to a ‘vulnerability assessment’ which identifies, quantifies, and prioritises vulnerabilities in a system. 

**Nicole:** Organisations often shift to different domains for business and technical reasons and may forget to renew their older domains but still use them in their code and infrastructure, opening themselves up to Domain takeover. The abandoned domains can offer attackers an opportunity to take advantage of this vulnerability. When an attacker registers the abandoned domain, they own the domain and its subdomains and other type of DNS records and can exploit them. Pentesters can look for potential domains that might exist and be vulnerable to takeover. Subdomain takeovers generally occur when a subdomain points to a service that no longer exists. An attacker may take it over but only that subdomain is affected.  We can see through looking at core terminology, that a Breach in security can be made by a range of unauthorised attacks, through a variety of different means, and can be on the whole much more damaging than a physical breach of security. Cyber criminals have potential access to everything and often it can be detected too late to mitigate the effects.
