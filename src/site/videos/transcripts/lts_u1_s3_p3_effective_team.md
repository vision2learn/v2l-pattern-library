What are the differences between an ineffective team and an effective team?

Ineffective teams are usually made up of people who don’t really work well together. 

Individuals within the team may show favouritism, put their own goals ahead of team goals, and deflect responsibility for their own mistakes. Some members may not even have the appropriate skills or too many members may have the same skills. Ineffective teams may also take actions before issues are examined or resolved, leading to unconstructive disagreements and members feeling vulnerable. 

Effective teams on the other hand are made up of people who have complimentary skills and can work together to accomplish the goals of the team. 

So, let’s have a look at behaviours that might be seen in effective teams in more detail. 

Safe to be vulnerable 

In an effective team, no one feels like they need to keep their guard up. That’s because they know that everyone is on the same side, working towards the same goals. They know their team will support them, encourage them, and help them grow and achieve. They also know they’re safe to express ideas without the fear of ridicule. 

For example, a new team member proposes an idea around tackling a difficult part of the current project, but they don’t realise this has been tried before and found not to work. The team won’t criticise or mock them for suggesting it, instead it’s used as a learning opportunity to explain why it didn’t work, and time is taken to thank them for their contribution. 

Ego-free zone 

In an effective team, people aren’t out for themselves, they’re there for the team. Although individual ambition should be encouraged, in an effective team a person’s ambitions are caught up in the success of their team. They put their team first and invest their energy and talent into achieving the team’s goals.

Constructive disagreements 

Team members will always have disagreements, but in an effective team, these should be about finding the right solution, and not about people just pushing their own agenda or opinion, for the sake of it. Disagreements and debates are respectful and polite, and personal feelings are kept to one side. The target is always about finding the best solution to allow the team to hit their goals. 

For example, during a tense part of a project, there are different possible pathways through. During a discussion about this, two team members in particular, have differing, but very passionate ideas about what will work. Although they’re forthright with one another, they still give each other time to fully explain their proposed idea so the team can decide. And, when an option is selected, the person whose idea wasn’t used is thanked for their contribution to the discussion, and they respect the team’s decision. 

Accountability 

In an effective team, people take responsibility for their actions. They don’t try and deflect or blame others if things go wrong, instead they acknowledge their mistakes openly. This is why ‘safe to be vulnerable’ is important. When a mistake happens, it’s safe to take ownership of it because everyone’s main concern is making things better, not handing out blame. 

Accountability also allows people to feel pride in their work when things go right, as long as they recognise the role of the team too.

Trust 

Members of an effective team trust one another. They know that everyone is there for the same reason and that everyone wants to work together. By showing one another trust, the team will show you trust too. 

Honesty and integrity 

An effective team is one where people hold themselves to the same standards that they hold their peers to. They’re honest about themselves, what they’re worried about and what they’re proud of. They don’t apply double standards or favouritism. They respect everyone’s achievements and help everyone through their trials. 

So, you can see effective teams create bonds and strong relationships between members, and understand the goals and targets expected of them. They work as a team, for the team to achieve their defined team goals, and not as individuals with agendas.
