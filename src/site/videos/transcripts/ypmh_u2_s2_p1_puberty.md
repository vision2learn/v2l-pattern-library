RHIANNON: Puberty can be a challenging time as hormones kick in and children develop new moods and feelings and the "emotional rollercoaster" they're on, can have real lasting psychological effects, The physical changes give rise to a variety of social and emotional changes as well, directly affecting the body and brain. They start to look and act differently, and an array of social influences further accelerate these social and emotional changes.

LEAH: As their bodies change, they may experience a new and unfamiliar set of social experiences and become interested in forming what can become intense, romantic, and sometimes sexualised relationships with others and they may be start to become treated by society differently. 

Those who mature quickly may be more popular than those who mature slowly. Adults may also view them differently expecting more adult behaviour, when they are still more or less a child.

RHIANNON: Body dissatisfaction has been linked to depression and anxiety particularly in children who do not match the ‘ideal body’. Young people who are overweight can have lower self-esteem than their peers and may look to weight control options, such as using diet pills, diuretics and laxatives. 

LEAH: Children and young people with poor body image are less likely to take part in physical activities due to worries about their appearance, this can therefore compound their possible health issues. Whilst those with good body image are less likely to diet or use alcohol or cigarettes, increasing the chance of more positive mental health and well-being.

RHIANNON: While body image concerns affect both boys and girls, more girls are likely to be dissatisfied with their appearance and their weight and more likely to consider going on a diet or having cosmetic surgery. Whereas boys are less likely but might be more inclined to look at using steroids to bulk up. These behaviours can be the start of further mental health problems.  

LEAH: Young women often report feeling a pressure to be thin, but to still maintain curves, especially when they see how ‘the ideal’ is portrayed on social media, therefore they may look to having procedures to enhance themselves. Young men often report pressure to be tall and more muscular so may hit the gym, start lifting wights and bulking up naturally or using steroids. 

RHIANNON: How young people develop their sense of ‘ideal’ appearance is varied. Young people themselves identify the media, pressure from family and friends, comparisons with peers, and personal factors like low self-esteem, feelings of depression, and a need for control, as important influences on their own body image.

We need to be mindful of all of these factors to potentially counter some of the negative affects they can have on children and young people’s mental health.
