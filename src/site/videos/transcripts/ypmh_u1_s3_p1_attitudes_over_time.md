Historically, Mental Health has often been misunderstood, originally being thought to be caused by lunar cycles or evil spirits. That was why terms like lunatics and ‘lunatic asylums’ were once used. 

The first hospital to look after those with mental health problems was London's Royal Bethlem Hospital commonly known as ‘Bedlam’ because of the chaos that was seen there. 

Those showing symptoms of ‘madness’ were locked away from society and very often left to die in squalid, inhumane conditions. 

In the 18th century regulation of ‘madhouses’ started and inspections started to take place. At the turn of the 19th century, King George lll’s insanity became widely reported and brought about more discussion on mental health and more asylums were built specifically for those with mental health problems.

People feared asylums as they knew they probably wouldn’t be released and at the turn of the Century, Victorian asylums were still using controversial treatments without knowledge of how they truly affected people. 

But authorities were starting to differentiate between those who were suffering from mental illness such as schizophrenia and those who were mentally disabled or had learning difficulties.

In the middle of the last century advances in psychiatry meant that antipsychotic medication became available, to help give people the chance to remain in their community rather than institutions. However, there was still a great deal of ignorance and much stigma around mental health.

Things changed with the closure of asylums in the 1980’s and many people who had been in institutions for much of their life, were now told that they would be cared for in their community, but it was clear that services within the community were inadequate. 

Local health authorities had to make suitable arrangements for individuals experiencing mental health conditions and were given a duty to assess an individual’s unique needs and circumstances and have an assessment and review process in place.

By the beginning of the 21st Century the trend towards community care became central in mental health service provision, and government initiatives have tried to ensure that mental health awareness is a key aspect of overall healthcare.

But one real change, is the shift in society’s attitudes over this time as people have become more accepting of mental health problems and more supportive of those with issues.
