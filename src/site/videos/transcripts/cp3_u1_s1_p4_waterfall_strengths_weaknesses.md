**Leah:** So, what are the strengths and weaknesses of Waterfall and when should it be used over other methodologies?

**Chris:** The Strengths are it’s easy to use and manage -Waterfall is very easy to use and learn as it is a linear approach which runs from one stage to the next until the project is complete. Each stage has certain deliverables and a review so the next stage wouldn’t happen until that stage has been signed off.

Suitable for certain types of projects that required a high degree of predictability- As all the planning along with strong supporting documentation that occurs at the beginning of a project, there is a high degree of predictability which is more suitable for large scale projects. 

Discipline is built in-Each project has specific start and end points to the project. It is at these points that the client and stakeholders are involved. The project isn’t started until the requirements and design has been signed off, so the deadlines and requirements are likely to be met.

Documentation-One of the key principles of ‘Waterfall’ is its strong supporting documentation which is required for each stage. This supports the team and project, less training requirements, supports future projects and supplies details to the stakeholders if needed.

**Leah:** However, the Weaknesses are it’s

Difficult to change- As Waterfall is a linear approach it does not have the flexibility of Agile as once a stage is complete, it is difficult to go back.

Software delivered at end of production-As the software/product is delivered at the end of the production there is a risk that it won’t meet stakeholder’s expectations.

Gathering accurate requirements can be challenging-The first stage is crucial and would need a Project Manager with effective communication skills as this is where the requirements from the customers and stakeholders would be gathered. A customer may not know what they want until later on in the project and this could cause issues in the process and have an impact on the whole project.

Large projects which have dependencies on external systems such as hardware, and where the requirements need to remain fixed with little tolerance for failure [or bugs] would use a Waterfall methodology. 

Examples of this include ‘payment gateways’, ‘military defence systems’, ‘construction’, ‘flight controls’, ‘manufacturing plants’ etc
