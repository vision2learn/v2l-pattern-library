**Abraham:** So, what exactly are networks and what do they do? Let’s look at these in more detail.

**Nicole:** A LAN or Local Area Network connects devices such as personal computers and workstations that share tools, data and programmes. These may be in homes, schools, hospitals and small businesses and may use an ethernet protocol to define how the information moves around the network. 

They are connected using a private addressing scheme as defined by the TCP/IP protocol. For increased speed and security, a LAN relies on wired connections, however, wireless connections are also used. 

LANs using wireless connections are known as Wireless Local Area Networks. The protocol used to share data is WiFi but they can also operate with Bluetooth. Data can transmit at a fast rate if the number of linked computers is limited. 

They can also connect anything from two computers to several hundreds. To connect more than a couple of machines a hub would be used, this would link to each machine in the network. Also, other devices can be linked to a LAN such as printers for everyone to use. To connect to the internet or a WAN a router would be needed rather than a hub. 

**Abraham:** A MAN, or Metropolitan Area Network covers a larger area than a LAN. It can connect various users who are situated in a specific geographical region. It can be found within a large University or Hospital campus, a telephone company or cable TV network. It may serve as an ISP [internet service provider]. The overall goal of a MAN is to develop the links between LANs. It is designed for high-speed connectivity and will use optical fibres and switches and routers. Switches perform 2 key functions, filtering data and managing connections.

**Nicole:** A WAN or Wide Area Network is a computer network that extends over a large specific geographical area. This may be a large city or a country or could be the size of the world if the internet was one entity. 

A WAN connects LANs to other LANs and to MANs using ISPs. WAN's can be public or private. An organisation could have a company WAN which covers small regional offices, the main head office and cloud services. Devices used to transmit data through WAN are optical fibres, microwaves, and satellites. 

WANs by the nature of their size often have to cross territorial boundaries and international borders and as so are subject to different legal jurisdictions which may in turn lead to network usage rights. Often also network cables run under the sea which can open them up to damage by weather, marine traffic, or sabotage.

Data sent over networks such as the internet is divided into packets. This means large amount of data is split up so that it doesn't slow down the system. It is sent in small packages through the wires, cables and radio waves of the internet. The data is then reconstituted in the correct order by the recipient’s device.

It is possible theoretically to send data over the internet without chopping it into small packets. It could be sent as a line of bits, but this approach is impractical when more than 2 computers are involved. 

**Abraham:** The main two ways in which packets are sent are TCP and UDP.

TCP ensures that all data packets arrive where they need to, with a handshake done to confirm. An example of this is web traffic – it’s important you get all the packets so that the website renders correctly.

UDP does not have a handshake and sends all packets and doesn’t care if they all arrive. An example of UDP traffic is live video streaming – you wouldn’t want the whole programme you’re watching to stop just because one packet representing a tiny part of the stream didn’t come through because of a network blip.

The internet is a packet switching network allowing packets to be processed independently from each other. As a result, billions of devices can exchange data at the same time.

Network traffic is a term that refers to packets however not all packets are good and not all network traffic is safe. Attackers can generate malicious network traffic designed to compromise or overwhelm a network in the form of DDoS.
