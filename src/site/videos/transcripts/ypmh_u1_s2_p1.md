RHIANNON: Legislation and guidance and the processes connected with them, are there to protect and support the rights of all children and young people and their families, and also provide support to those who work with children and young people with mental health issues.

LEAH: Whether in the home, at school or in a mental health setting, a child or young person should never be treated in a way that is inhumane or degrading. They should always be protected from abuse or neglect that can cause mental or physical harm. 

RHIANNON: The family and by extension, family life, should also be respected at all times. For example, sending a child or young person to another part of the country for residential care and support makes it harder for family visits and maintaining those important relationships which can be distressing for all concerned. 

LEAH: Wherever possible a young person should be consulted about their care and be involved in the decision making. If the child is deemed not legally competent, consent should be obtained from someone with parental responsibility, unless in the case of an emergency.

RHIANNON: Parental responsibility should be involved in all important decision making in relation to a child, for example: 
- where the child goes to school, 
- appointing a guardian in the event of the loss of a parent, 
- consenting to medical treatment 
- and accessing a child’s medical records. 

LEAH: This is also the case for legal matters, and religious or cultural issues until the child reaches an age to make their own decisions. 
 
Let’s now look at how data is used and protected.

RHIANNON: Any data held on children and young adults comes under the 'data protection principles' and that information must be used fairly, lawfully and transparently. It should always be up to date, relevant and limited to only what is necessary. 

LEAH: Any information no longer needed, should be then destroyed securely. For example, childcare providers and even child minders need to comply with data protection for every child in their care, even if that is for a limited time.

RHIANNON: With further advances in digital footprints, there is now stronger legal protection for online identification markers, location data, genetic information, biometrics and more, and there are separate safeguards for personal data which relate to criminal convictions and offences.

LEAH: The Data Protection Act, means an individual has the right to know what information the government and other organisations store about them, including, the right to be informed about how it is being used, the ability to have access to it and have it corrected or erased when appropriate.

RHIANNON: Children especially, require specific protection regarding their data as they may not be fully aware of the risks or consequences of processing personal data or their rights. 

LEAH: With so much online communication, children and young people are constantly putting their data in the public domain by signing up to the various forms of social media and are not always aware of the consequences. 

RHIANNON: Adults with parental responsibility should endeavour to monitor that data is being used correctly, whist still respecting the rights of the child or young person. 

LEAH: We must remember legislation and guidance is there to protect and should be used accordingly.
