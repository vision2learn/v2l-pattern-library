In the past children and young people who showed signs of severe mental illness such as Schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, were thought to be not able to function properly throughout life and may never successfully contribute to society. However, this is not the case when handled appropriately with individuals being involved in their own treatment.

 
RHIANNON:  
The recovery model is familiar to most mental health practitioners and psychologists, psychiatrists, doctors, and other health professionals can provide such support to some degree. However, the connections and support offered by family, friends, and other peers can also be critical, along with support groups and the community. 

 
In particular, the recovery model stresses the importance of connectedness and social supports. When children and young people have supportive relationships that offer unconditional love, they are better able to cope with the symptoms of their illness and work towards recovery.

 
LEAH:  
The recovery model of mental illness is often contrasted against what is known as the medical model. The medical model focuses on mental disorders having physiological causes, with treatment often focused on the use of medications.

 
The two different models are often presented as being in opposition to one another, but they can be complementary and can be used together.

 
RHIANNON:  
While the medical model ensures that physiological causes are fully addressed, the recovery model ensures that individuals are able to be directly involved in their own treatment, offering them personal empowerment, and the family or peer support needed to cope with their illness and help them work towards getting better. 

 
LEAH:  
One of the recovery model’s major benefits is that it focuses on the individual’s strengths and abilities in knowing their own experiences and to be able to take an active role in their treatment.
