**Chris:** We have looked at the various risks that may affect a project, but what can you do to reduce risks and carry out risk mitigation? 

It’s worth considering that when a project is still at a very early stage there will many unknowns and some may even impact getting the project started. 

So how can you ensure that risks are reduced or mitigated? Let’s look at what steps you can take.

**Leah:** • Identify potential risks 

• Evaluate the risks identified and prioritise 

• Plan how the risks that have been identified can be managed or mitigated using Agile methodology

• Undertake regular reviews to evaluate if the identified risks are likely to occur and if so, implement appropriate actions to mitigate the impact.

• Monitor for any new risks that may arise

• Provide updates to stakeholders on the status of existing risks and any new risks that may potentially arise. 

**Chris:** Let’s now look at how some of the most common risks can be mitigated.

Missing or unclear requirements

A failure to capture and fully understand all the requirements of the project is a poor foundation for success. Ideally everyone involved will be clear on exactly what the requirements are, and this reinforces the importance of planning. However, if requirements are missing or unclear, using Agile methodology can help in mitigating risks. By going for an iterative development cycle and meeting with customers/clients at every stage of the development process on completion of each iteration, team members can show stakeholders the progress made and review the results so ensuring relevancy. 

**Leah:** Skills shortage

Skilled software developers are always in demand and if people leave mid project, it can directly impact the Agile methodology being used, especially if it is the ‘project owner’ or ‘scrum master’. As the team will be working in ‘sprints’, the amount of work to be completed in the ‘sprint timeframe’ will have been agreed, and the loss of one or more individuals may result in failure of the ‘sprint goal’ impacting the timeframe of the project. 

It may also be that the project requires new skills to be learnt which will have an impact but identifying this early in the planning process can help resolve this in a timely manner. 

Looking at the skillsets of others within the organisation can help identify where that skills shortage can be filled. A flexible and multiskilled workforce offers the opportunity to draw on other teams or individuals if and when required. 

**Chris:** Time delays

Running out of time is always a constant threat on any project. This is far more damaging in certain methodologies such as ‘Waterfall’ or ‘Kanban’ which follow a linear approach, where one stage has to be completed before the next begins. By using an ‘Agile methodology’ such as ‘scrum’ several stages are being worked on at the same time, so delays to one task will not impact the completion of the others. 

**Leah:** Over budget

A project may run over budget for several reasons like issues within the development team or perhaps more likely to do with client requirements.

Clients may change the requirement mid project or there may be new technological reasons why changes have to be made or new features added. Working in Agile such as scrum, can mitigate this as it gives the opportunity to take a look at the projects scope and see if there is any opportunity to reduce this, but still meet requirements. This can give a deliverable product meeting requirements in the short term that can be developed into versioning, so changes may be made in later releases.

**Chris:** Resource shortages

Resource shortages especially human resources can be a major risk. Resources may often be being used by other teams. When planning, working out the resources required can be difficult, but forecasting possible resource risks is important. Creating a ‘resource allocation plan’ and analysing it to see potential risks and disruptions, can enable the project manager to build in mitigation. Some critical tasks may need to be completed by one specific individual, what if they are unavailable? To mitigate any impact, other team members can be upskilled to ensure risk is minimised. 

‘Capacity planning’ is a way to ensure that the organisation has sufficient resources for your project, identifying what resources are available compared to demand, and if required, increasing or re-allocating them. 

**Leah:** Decision making

Although it may be assumed the project manager is the one making the decisions, when working in an Agile environment such as Scrum, the team members are also responsible for making decisions. 

Part of creating a well-functioning team involves giving members the autonomy needed for the project. Scrum pushes the level of decision making downward, team members need to feel they have the autonomy, to become truly invested in the project. Using Jira boards or project boards in GitHub can help show any potential bottlenecks or issues arising, which makes decision making easier, as these issues will need to be resolved.
