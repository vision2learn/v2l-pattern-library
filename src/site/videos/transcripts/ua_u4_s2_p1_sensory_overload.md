BETH: Sensory overload happens when there is more input from the five senses than a brain can sort through and process. These could be; multiple conversations, flashing overhead lights, lots of music or loud noise. 

In most people the brain prioritises what sensory information to focus on, but with individuals in autism this is not straightforward.

JOE: Individuals with autism may find this more of a problem because they may suffer from hypersensitivity and therefore things that might not normally cause sensory overload can seem much worse.  

Their brain focusses on something such as a buzzing sound and sends a message to get away from it. The body then starts to panic, and a chain reaction can start.

Sensory overload in people with autism can mean focusing on the smallest most insignificant thing over other things. It may be flashing lights, a fluffy carpet, marks on a wall, or even a strong smell.

BETH: Symptoms of sensory overload may vary but some common ones include difficulty focusing, extreme irritability or restlessness and discomfort. An individual may need to cover their eyes or ears to shield themselves from any sensory input. They may become agitated and stressed about their surrounding and their sensitivity to other things may increase. 

Eventually sensory overload can cause extreme stress and anxiety, physical pain and distressed behaviour which may develop into a meltdown.
